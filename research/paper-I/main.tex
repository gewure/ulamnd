\begin{abstract}
How many primes lie among the values $f(1),f(2),f(3),\dots$ of a polynomial, and how much does that
number fluctuate? The first question has a conjectural answer, the Bateman--Horn constant $C(f)$; this
paper is about the second, which for the sequence of all primes is the question that leads to the zeros
of the Riemann zeta function. The fluctuation is governed by an explicit arithmetic function, the pair
singular series $S_f(h)$, the Euler product that under the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture weights the
simultaneous primality of $f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$; all our theorems concern $S_f$ and involve no prime. We
prove an exact identity for $\sum_{h\le H}(S_f(h)-C(f)^2)$ in terms of the roots of $f$ modulo squarefree
$d$; that its diagonal part has Dirichlet series $\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$, $K=\Q[t]/(f)$, with residue exactly
$1/C(f)$ and $1/E_f$ essentially the Artin $L$-function of the symmetric square of the permutation
representation on the roots, so that, under the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture, the Dedekind zeta function of the
field cut out by $f$ governs the second moment of its prime values; a closed formula for $S_f(h)$ when $f$ is quadratic; and that the
asymptotic
\[
\sum_{h\le H}\Bigl(1-\frac hH\Bigr)\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)=-\tfrac12\,C(f)\log H+O_f(1)
\]
holds unconditionally for linear $f$ and, for general $f$, exactly when the sum over pairs of distinct
roots is $o(H\log H)$. We conjecture the asymptotic for every $f$, with the \emph{first} power of $C(f)$
rather than the second that a naive rescaling predicts; $\exN$ quadratics computed exactly to $H=10^6$
give the exponent $\exCesAlpha\pm\exCesAlphaSe$ and the coefficient $\exCesK$ against the predicted $\tfrac12$. In plain terms, and conjecturally: primes along any polynomial sequence fluctuate
less than independent coin flips would, by the mechanism that Montgomery and Soundararajan found for
primes in short intervals, and the size of the effect is set by an $L$-function attached to the
polynomial. Assuming the Hardy--Littlewood pair conjecture, the law $\Var/\E\to1-\log T/\log X$ therefore
holds for the prime values of every polynomial, with a polynomial-dependent correction that is large at
accessible $T$, as prime counts along $\nCells$ polynomial sequences confirm against a random-integer
control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

For an irreducible polynomial $f\in\Q[t]$ that is integer-valued, has positive leading coefficient and
no fixed prime divisor, the Bateman--Horn conjecture~\cite{BatemanHorn1962} (Conjecture~F of Hardy and
Littlewood~\cite{HardyLittlewood1923} for quadratics; see~\cite{Aletheia2020} for a survey) predicts
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BH}
\#\{t<T:\ f(t)\ \text{prime}\}\sim C(f)\sum_{t<T}\frac1{\log f(t)},\qquad
C(f)=\prod_p\frac{1-\omega_f(p)/p}{1-1/p},
\end{equation}
where $\omega_f(p)$ is the number of roots of $f$ modulo $p$ (for primes dividing the denominator of
$f$, $\omega_f(p)/p$ denotes the density of $t$ with $p\mid f(t)$). The object of this paper is not the
count of primes but an arithmetic function attached to $f$. For $h\ge1$ let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S}
S_f(h)=\prod_p\frac{1-\omega_{f,2}(p,h)/p}{(1-1/p)^2},\qquad
\omega_{f,2}(p,h)=2\omega_f(p)-\nu_f(p,h),
\end{equation}
where $\nu_f(p,h)=\#\{t\bmod p:\ p\mid f(t)\text{ and }p\mid f(t+h)\}$ is the number of roots $s$ of
$f$ modulo $p$ such that $s+h$ is again a root (with the same convention for primes dividing the
denominator). This is the \emph{pair singular series} of Hardy and Littlewood: heuristically,
$S_f(h)/(\log f(t)\log f(t+h))$ is the probability that $f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$ are both prime, and for
$f(t)=t$ it is the twin-prime singular series $\Sig(h)$. But $S_f(h)$ is also, in itself, an explicit
and exactly computable function of $h$: an absolutely convergent Euler product, equal to $C(f)^2$ times
a product over the primes dividing a resultant, and for quadratics a finite product of twin-prime type
(Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic}). Everything we prove concerns this function and no prime. Its mean value is
$C(f)^2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion} gives $\sum_{h\le H}S_f(h)=C(f)^2H+O(\log H)$), and the question is
the second-order term. Our main proposal is:

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj}
For every irreducible, integer-valued $f$ without fixed prime divisor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conj}
\sum_{h\le H}\Bigl(1-\frac hH\Bigr)\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)=-\tfrac12\,C(f)\log H+A_f+o(1)\qquad(H\to\infty),
\end{equation}
with a constant $A_f$ depending on $f$.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture is about the Ces\`aro mean, and only about it. We write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conjsharp}
\Sigma_f(H):=\sum_{h\le H}\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)
\end{equation}
for the sharp sum, of which the left side of~\eqref{eq:conj} is the average $\frac1H\sum_{H'<H}\Sigma_f(H')$, and we
make no conjecture about $\Sigma_f(H)$ itself beyond what follows from~\eqref{eq:conj} by partial summation.

\begin{remark}[the sharp sum has no bounded remainder]\label{rem:sharp}
Earlier versions of this paper stated, as a consequence of~\eqref{eq:conj}, that
$\Sigma_f(H)=-\tfrac12C(f)\log H+O_f(1)$. That is false, and it is not a consequence of anything: differencing gives
$\Sigma_f(H)-\Sigma_f(H-1)=S_f(H)-C(f)^2$, so a bounded remainder would force $S_f$ to be bounded, whereas $S_f(h)$
is unbounded. For $f=t$ this is classical: $\Sig(H)=2C_2\prod_{p\mid H,\,p>2}\frac{p-1}{p-2}$ grows like a constant
times $\log\log H$ along the primorials $H=\prod_{p\le y}p$ ($\Sig(H)/2C_2=5.44$ at $y=47$, where $\log\log H=3.7$).
For $f=t^2+1$ the same growth comes from the primes represented by $Q(h)=h^2+4$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic}, that
is, from the off-diagonal: choosing $h$ even with $p\mid h^2+4$ for the first $K$ split primes $p$ (and $h\equiv1$
modulo the small inert primes, so that no prime dividing $h$ contributes) gives
$S_f(h)/C(f)^2=2.70,\,2.93,\,3.14,\,3.36,\,3.56$ for $K=5,10,20,40,80$, against $1.12$ for $K=0$. The jumps of
$\Sigma_f(H)$ therefore grow like $\log\log H$, and two consecutive values of $\Sigma_f$ cannot both stay within a
bounded distance of $-\tfrac12C\log H$. What is true is weaker and of a different kind: for $f=t$,
$\sum_{h\le H}\Sig(h)=H-\tfrac12\log H+O((\log H)^{2/3})$ (Friedlander--Goldston, Vaughan), and the correct order of
that remainder is open. Between the parity drop, which for $f=t^2+1$ takes $\Sigma_f$ down by exactly $C(f)^2$ at
every odd $H$ because $S_f$ vanishes at odd $h$, and this slow unbounded growth, the sharp sum is the wrong object.
The Ces\`aro mean averages both effects out; it is what the exact identity of Section~\ref{sec:sum} controls
(Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed}) and what the data of Section~\ref{sec:sum} measure. The constant $A_f$ is defined
by~\eqref{eq:conj}, and the numerical constants ``$A_f/C$'' of Section~\ref{sec:sum} are Ces\`aro constants. (The
bounded-remainder claim was pointed out to us by an external assessment of the repository on 12 September 2026; see
\texttt{research/ERRATA.md}, item~11.)
\end{remark}

For $f(t)=t$ this is a theorem: $\sum_{h\le H}\Sig(h)=H-\tfrac12\log H+O((\log H)^{2/3})$, due to
Friedlander and Goldston~\cite{FriedlanderGoldston1995} and Vaughan~\cite{Vaughan2001} after
Montgomery and Soundararajan~\cite{MontgomerySoundararajan2004}, who derived from it, assuming a
uniform Hardy--Littlewood conjecture, that the number of primes in an interval of length $H$ near $X$
has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MS}
\frac{\Var}{\E}\approx 1-\frac{\log H}{\log X}.
\end{equation}
(Gallagher~\cite{Gallagher1976} had shown that the prime-tuple conjectures imply Poisson statistics in
intervals of length $\lambda\log X$, and Goldston and Montgomery~\cite{GoldstonMontgomery1987} that,
under the Riemann Hypothesis, the asymptotic variance for $H=X^\theta$ is equivalent to Montgomery's
pair correlation conjecture for the zeros of $\zeta$~\cite{Montgomery1973}; in the function-field setting
the variance statements are theorems~\cite{KeatingRudnick2014}.) The point of Conjecture~\ref{conj} is
the \emph{first} power of $C(f)$: the second-order term is not $C(f)^2$ times the linear case, as a
naive rescaling of the twin-prime heuristic would give, but $C(f)$ times it.

\paragraph{Why this matters, in plain terms.} A polynomial sequence such as $t^2+1$ is expected to
contain primes with density $C(f)/\log f(t)$, as if each value were an independent coin flip with that
probability. Independent coin flips would make the number of primes among the first $T$ values fluctuate
with variance equal to its mean. Primes are not independent: whether $f(t)$ is divisible by a small prime
$p$ repeats with period $p$ in $t$, so the sieving by small primes is deterministic along the sequence and
only the large primes contribute randomness. The function $S_f(h)$ measures the resulting correlation
between $f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$, and the sum $\sum_{h\le H}(S_f(h)-C^2)$ is the total correlation up to
distance $H$: it is negative, and under the pair conjecture it is the leading correction by which the
variance falls short of the coin-flip value. We conjecture that this shortfall is $\tfrac12C(f)\log H$ for
every polynomial, with the \emph{first} power of $C(f)$. For the linear polynomial this is the classical
statement, going back to Montgomery and Soundararajan, that primes in short intervals are ``more regular
than random''; we conjecture that the same regularity, with the same constant $\tfrac12$, holds along every
polynomial, and we prove the mechanism that produces it: the diagonal part of the sum has the Dedekind
zeta function of the field defined by $f$ as its generating function, with residue exactly $1/C(f)$. Two
gaps remain, and they are of different kinds. The conjecture itself is reduced to, but not proved from, an
equidistribution statement about the roots of $f$ modulo $d$ (Hypothesis~(E) below); and the translation
from $S_f$ to prime counts assumes the Hardy--Littlewood pair conjecture, which is open for every
non-linear $f$.

\paragraph{Results.} Section~\ref{sec:sum} proves an exact identity for the left side
of~\eqref{eq:conj} (Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion}), identifies the Dirichlet series of its diagonal part
as $\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$ with residue $1/C(f)$ at $s=0$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet}), which is where
the first power of $C(f)$ comes from, identifies $E_f$ as the inverse of the Artin $L$-function of the
symmetric square of the permutation representation on the roots (Theorem~\ref{thm:artin}), gives a
closed twin-prime-type formula for $S_f(h)$ when $f$ is quadratic (Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic}), proves
the Ces\`aro form of~\eqref{eq:conj} for every linear $f$ and reduces it for general $f$ to a bound
$o(H\log H)$ for the off-diagonal sum over pairs of distinct roots of $f$ modulo $d$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed}; the constant term needs the sharper Hypothesis~(E)), tests the conjecture
numerically for $\sumsN$ polynomials of degree $1$--$4$ up to $H=10^5$ and, exactly and with $C(f)$
varying over more than a decade, for $\exN$ quadratics up to $H=10^6$ (Tables~\ref{tab:sums},
\ref{tab:exact}, Figures~\ref{fig:curves}, \ref{fig:exact}), isolates the off-diagonal remainder for
quadratics (Table~\ref{tab:off}), and proves that over $\F_q[u]$ the off-diagonal contribution of every
modulus of degree $\le N$ vanishes identically, the remainder being a finite sum over moduli of degree
between $N$ and about $3N$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ff}, Lemma~\ref{lem:support}). Section~\ref{sec:riesz}
draws a consequence: for a quadratic the diagonal Riesz means have an explicit formula over the zeros of
$\zeta^2L(\chi_D)$ (Proposition~\ref{thm:explicit}), explains why these zeros are nevertheless invisible
in a direct computation to $4\cdot10^7$, and tests whether the Galois group affects the constant.

\paragraph{Consequences for primes.} Let $\mathbf 1_t$ be the indicator that $f(t)$ is prime,
$p_t=\min(1,C(f)/\log f(t))$ its Bateman--Horn probability, $A_T=\sum_{t<T}\mathbf 1_t$ and
$V_T=\sum_{t<T}p_t(1-p_t)$ the independent-trials variance. If the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture holds for
the pair $(f(t),f(t+h))$, i.e.\ $\Pr[\mathbf 1_t\mathbf 1_{t+h}=1]\approx S_f(h)\,w_tw_{t+h}$ with
$w_t=1/\log f(t)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:varHL}
\Var_{\mathrm{HL}}(A_T)=V_T+2\sum_{h=1}^{T-1}\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)\,W_T(h),\qquad W_T(h)=\sum_{t<T-h}w_tw_{t+h},
\end{equation}
and inserting~\eqref{eq:conj} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\frac{\Var}{\E}\approx1-\frac{\log T}{\log X}+\frac{1+2\bar A_f(T)/C(f)}{\log X},\qquad X\approx f(T),
\end{equation}
where $\bar A_f(T)$ is the average over $H<T$ of $\Sigma_f(H)+\tfrac12C\log H$ (it tends to $A_f$).
So the Montgomery--Soundararajan law~\eqref{eq:MS} holds asymptotically for every polynomial, with a
correction that depends on $f$ only through $A_f/C(f)$; but $\Sigma_f(H)$ approaches its asymptotic form
slowly, $\bar A_f(T)/C$ is of order $-1$ to $-2$ for $T\le10^3$, and the correction is a negative
$0.1$--$0.2$ at the lengths one can compute. Section~\ref{sec:primes} compares this with primes along
$\nCells$ cells of polynomial sequences, with a random-integer control.

\paragraph{What is conditional on what.} Three statements of very different status appear in this
paper, and we separate them. (A)~The Bateman--Horn conjecture~\eqref{eq:BH} for a non-linear $f$, i.e.\
that $f$ takes infinitely many prime values at the predicted density. It is not known for a single
non-linear polynomial; for $t^2+1$ it is one of Landau's problems of 1912, and the obstruction is the
parity barrier of sieve theory. No theorem of this paper depends on it. (B)~The Hardy--Littlewood pair
conjecture for $f$, which is stronger than~(A). It is used only in~\eqref{eq:varHL} and
Section~\ref{sec:primes}, to translate Conjecture~\ref{conj} into a statement about the variance of prime
counts; the paper of Montgomery and Soundararajan is conditional in exactly the same way. (C)~Conjecture~\ref{conj}
itself, and the Hypothesis~(E) to which Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed} reduces it. These are statements about
the explicit arithmetic function $S_f$, provable or refutable with no conjecture about primes anywhere;
Hypothesis~(E) is a concrete equidistribution statement about the roots of $f$ modulo $d$ on average
over $d$, in the family of the theorems of Hooley~\cite{Hooley1963} and of Duke, Friedlander and
Iwaniec~\cite{DFI1995} on the roots of quadratic congruences. The theorems of Section~\ref{sec:sum} are
unconditional; Proposition~\ref{thm:explicit} assumes the Riemann hypothesis for $\zeta$ and
$L(s,\chi_D)$ and a contour argument that we have sketched. Section~\ref{sec:spiral} records where our polynomial families come from (a
$d$-dimensional Ulam spiral whose lattice lines carry polynomials of degree exactly $d$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:poly}). Section~\ref{sec:disc} discusses what is proved, what is heuristic, and what
should be done next.

\section{The pair singular series}\label{sec:pair}

We record the elementary properties of $S_f$ that are used below. Write $R(h)$ for the resultant of $f(t)$
and $f(t+h)$ (up to the denominators of $f$), a nonzero integer for $h\ne0$ since $f$ is irreducible and
not constant. For $p\nmid R(h)$ the polynomials $f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$ have no common root modulo $p$, so
$\nu_f(p,h)=0$ and the local factor of~\eqref{eq:S} equals $(1-2\omega_f(p)/p)/(1-1/p)^2=(1-\omega_f(p)/p)^2(1-1/p)^{-2}\cdot P_p$
with $P_p=(1-2\omega_f(p)/p)/(1-\omega_f(p)/p)^2=1-\omega_f(p)^2/(p-\omega_f(p))^2$; since
$\sum_p\omega_f(p)^2/p^2<\infty$, the product converges absolutely and
\[
S_f(h)=C(f)^2\prod_pP_p\prod_{p\mid R(h)}\frac{1+g_p(h)}{P_p},\qquad
1+g_p(h)=\frac{1-\omega_{f,2}(p,h)/p}{(1-\omega_f(p)/p)^2},
\]
a finite correction to the constant $C(f)^2\prod_pP_p$. In particular $S_f(h)\ge0$, with $S_f(h)=0$
exactly when some prime divides $f(t)f(t+h)$ for every $t$. For $f(t)=t$ one has $\omega_f=1$,
$\nu_f(p,h)=\mathbf 1_{p\mid h}$, and $S_f=\Sig$ is the twin-prime singular series
$2\prod_{p>2}(1-(p-1)^{-2})\prod_{p\mid h,p>2}\frac{p-1}{p-2}$ for even $h$. For a general $f$ the
correction factors depend on $h$ through the residues of the differences of roots of $f$ modulo $p$, and
Section~\ref{sec:sum} makes this dependence exact. The pair-correlation interpretation of $S_f$, and the
prime counts it predicts, are taken up in Section~\ref{sec:primes}. The Bateman--Horn conjecture is
open for every non-linear $f$, but Kravitz, Woo and Xu~\cite{KravitzWooXu2025} have shown that a
polynomial analogue of the prime-tuples conjecture holds for $100\%$ of polynomials in an $L^2$ sense,
and Banks and Ford~\cite{BanksFord2026} that random sets of the right density satisfy Bateman--Horn
statistics almost surely.

\section{The sum of the pair singular series}\label{sec:sum}

\emph{This section contains the theorems of the paper. The idea is simple: expand $S_f(h)$ into a sum
over moduli $d$ of terms that depend on $h$ only through $h\bmod d$, sum over $h\le H$, and read off the
secondary term from the moduli whose contribution does not average out. The pairs of equal roots of $f$
modulo $d$ give a Dirichlet series with a pole whose residue is $1/C(f)$; the pairs of distinct roots
are the obstacle.}

Throughout this section $f\in\Z[t]$ is irreducible of degree $n\ge1$, with positive leading
coefficient and no fixed prime divisor, so that $\omega_f(p)<p$ for every $p$; $R(h)$ denotes the
resultant of $f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$, a non-zero integer for $h\ne0$ (two roots of an irreducible
polynomial never differ by a non-zero integer), and $K=\Q[t]/(f)$. (The spiral polynomials of
Section~\ref{sec:spiral} have denominators dividing $d!$; everything below goes through with the
period $p^{v_p(\mathrm{den})+1}$ in place of $p$ at the finitely many primes dividing the denominator,
as implemented in the code.)

\subsection{An exact identity}\label{sec:heur}

For squarefree $q$ let $\rho_q(a)=\sum_{s\bmod q,\ f(s)\equiv0}e(as/q)$ be the exponential sum over the
roots of $f$ modulo $q$, and define the \emph{root Ramanujan sum}
\[
c^f_q(h)=\sum_{\substack{a\bmod q\\(a,q)=1}}|\rho_q(a)|^2\,e(-ah/q)=\sum_{s,s'\bmod q}c_q(s'-s-h),
\]
where $c_q$ is the classical Ramanujan sum; $c^f_q$ is multiplicative in $q$ by the Chinese remainder
theorem, and $c^f_p(h)=p\,\nu_f(p,h)-\omega_f(p)^2$. For $f(t)=t$ one has $c^f_q=c_q$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:expansion}
Let $b(q)=\prod_{p\mid q}(p-\omega_f(p))^{-2}$. For every $h\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expansion}
\frac{S_f(h)}{C(f)^2}=\sum_{q\ \mathrm{squarefree}}b(q)\,c^f_q(h),
\end{equation}
the series converging absolutely. Consequently, for every $H\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:identity}
\sum_{h\le H}\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)=C(f)^2\sum_{d\ge2}W_f(d)\,\Psi_d(H),\qquad
W_f(d)=d\,b(d)\prod_{p\nmid d}\Bigl(1-\frac{\omega_f(p)^2}{(p-\omega_f(p))^2}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $d$ runs over squarefree integers,
$\Psi_d(H)=\sum_{s,s'}\psi_d(s'-s,H)$, the sum over pairs of roots of $f$ modulo $d$, with
$\psi_d(m,H)=\#\{h\le H:\ h\equiv m\ (d)\}-H/d$, and the series converges absolutely.
Splitting the pairs $(s,s')$ into $s=s'$ and $s\ne s'$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
\sum_{h\le H}\bigl(S_f(h)-C^2\bigr)=-C^2\sum_{d\ge2}a_f(d)\Bigl\{\frac Hd\Bigr\}+C^2\,\mathrm{Off}_f(H),
\qquad a_f(d)=W_f(d)\,\omega_f(d),
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{Off}_f(H)=\sum_{d}W_f(d)\sum_{s\ne s'}\psi_d(s'-s,H)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $a_p=\omega_f(p)/p$. By~\eqref{eq:S} and~\eqref{eq:BH},
$S_f(h)/C^2=\prod_p(1+g_p(h))$ with
$g_p(h)=(\nu_f(p,h)/p-a_p^2)/(1-a_p)^2=c^f_p(h)/(p-\omega_f(p))^2$. For $p\nmid R(h)$ one has
$\nu_f(p,h)=0$, so $g_p(h)=-a_p^2/(1-a_p)^2=O(p^{-2})$ and $\sum_p|g_p(h)|<\infty$; hence the product
converges absolutely and equals $\sum_q\prod_{p\mid q}g_p(h)$, which is~\eqref{eq:expansion} by
multiplicativity. Summing over $h\le H$ (a finite sum) and using
$\sum_{h\le H}c_q(m-h)=\sum_{d\mid q}d\,\mu(q/d)\,\#\{h\le H:\ d\mid m-h\}$, we get for $q>1$
\[
\sum_{h\le H}c^f_q(h)=\sum_{d\mid q}d\,\mu(q/d)\sum_{s,s'\bmod q}\Bigl(\frac Hd+\psi_d(s'-s,H)\Bigr)
=\sum_{d\mid q,\ d>1}d\,\mu(q/d)\,\omega_f(q/d)^2\,\Psi_d(H),
\]
because $\sum_{d\mid q}\mu(q/d)=0$ and pairs of roots modulo $q$ project onto pairs modulo $d$ with
multiplicity $\omega_f(q/d)^2$. Writing $q=dm$ with $(m,d)=1$ and summing over $m$,
$\sum_{(m,d)=1}\mu(m)b(m)\omega_f(m)^2=\prod_{p\nmid d}(1-\omega_f(p)^2/(p-\omega_f(p))^2)$, an
absolutely convergent product; this gives~\eqref{eq:identity}. The rearrangement is justified because
$\sum_d d\,b(d)|\Psi_d(H)|<\infty$: for fixed $h$, $\nu_f(d,h)\ne0$ forces $d\mid R(h)$ up to a bounded
factor, so $\sum_d d\,b(d)\sum_{h\le H}\nu_f(d,h)$ is a finite sum, while
$\sum_d d\,b(d)\,\omega_f(d)^2H/d<\infty$. Finally $\psi_d(0,H)=-\{H/d\}$ gives~\eqref{eq:split}.
\end{proof}

The diagonal part of~\eqref{eq:split} is a sum of fractional parts weighted by the multiplicative
function $a_f$; the off-diagonal part collects, for every $d$, the discrepancies of the residue classes
$s'-s$ of \emph{differences of distinct roots} of $f$ modulo $d$, each of size $O(1)$ and of mean zero
over the residues. For linear $f$ there are no distinct roots and $\mathrm{Off}_f\equiv0$.

\subsection{The diagonal: the Dedekind zeta function and the residue $1/C(f)$}\label{sec:diag}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:dirichlet}
The Dirichlet series $D_f(s)=\sum_d a_f(d)d^{-s}$ has the Euler product
$D_f(s)=\prod_p\bigl(P_p+p\,b(p)\,\omega_f(p)\,p^{-s}\bigr)$ with $P_p=1-\omega_f(p)^2/(p-\omega_f(p))^2$,
and
\[
D_f(s)=\zeta_K(s+1)\,E_f(s)
\]
with $E_f$ holomorphic in $\Re s>-\tfrac12$. In particular $D_f$ is meromorphic there with a single
simple pole at $s=0$, and
\[
\operatorname*{Res}_{s=0}D_f(s)=\prod_p\frac{1-1/p}{1-\omega_f(p)/p}=\frac1{C(f)},\qquad
\sum_{d\le x}a_f(d)=\frac{\log x}{C(f)}+B_f+O\Bigl(\frac1{\log x}\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$a_f$ is supported on squarefree $d$ with $a_f(d)=\prod_{p\mid d}p\,b(p)\omega_f(p)\prod_{p\nmid d}P_p$,
which gives the Euler product. At $s=0$ the local factor is
$P_p+p\,b(p)\omega_f(p)=\bigl((p-\omega)^2-\omega^2+p\omega\bigr)/(p-\omega)^2=p/(p-\omega_f(p))$. For
large $p$, $P_p+p\,b(p)\omega_f(p)p^{-s}=1+\omega_f(p)p^{-1-s}+O(p^{-2})$ uniformly in $\Re s\ge-\tfrac12+\delta$,
while for every $p$ not dividing the discriminant of $f$ the Euler factor of $\zeta_K(s+1)$ is
$1+\omega_f(p)p^{-1-s}+O(p^{-2-2\Re s})$ by the Dedekind--Kummer theorem ($\omega_f(p)$ is then the
number of degree-one prime ideals above $p$).
The quotient is therefore $1+O(p^{-2+2\delta})$ and $E_f$ is an absolutely convergent Euler product, holomorphic
in $\Re s>-\tfrac12$. Since $\zeta_K$ has a simple pole at $1$, so has $D_f$ at $0$, and
$\operatorname*{Res}_{s=0}D_f=\lim_{s\to0^+}sD_f(s)=\lim_{s\to0^+}\prod_p(P_p+pb(p)\omega_f(p)p^{-s})(1-p^{-1-s})$;
the logarithm of the product is $\sum_p(\omega_f(p)-1)p^{-1-s}+(\text{absolutely convergent})$, and
$\sum_p(\omega_f(p)-1)p^{-1-s}$ converges at $s=0$ by the prime ideal theorem ($\sum_{p\le x}(\omega_f(p)-1)=o(x/\log x)$),
hence uniformly for $s\ge0$, so the limit is the value at $s=0$, $\prod_p\frac{p}{p-\omega_f(p)}(1-\frac1p)=1/C(f)$.
The asymptotic for $\sum_{d\le x}a_f(d)$ follows from the Selberg--Delange method~\cite[II.5]{Tenenbaum2015}
applied to $\sum_d d\,a_f(d)\,d^{-s}=D_f(s-1)=\zeta(s)\cdot\frac{\zeta_K(s)}{\zeta(s)}E_f(s-1)$, whose second
factor is holomorphic and non-vanishing in a neighbourhood of $s=1$ and in a standard zero-free region,
followed by partial summation.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet} is the rigorous content of the phrase ``the correlation carried by a prime
$p$ dividing $h$ has weight $\omega_f(p)/(p-\omega_f(p))$'': the partial sums of that weight grow like
$\log H/C(f)$, exactly as $\sum_{k\le H}\mu(k)^2/\varphi(k)\sim\log H$ does in the
Montgomery--Soundararajan computation~\cite{MontgomerySoundararajan2004} for $f(t)=t$. It also exhibits
the Dedekind zeta function of the field cut out by $f$ as the generating function of the diagonal.

\subsection{The Artin $L$-function of the symmetric square, and quadratics in closed form}\label{sec:artin}

The factor $E_f$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet} is not just ``holomorphic near $0$'': it is itself an
$L$-function, and this is what connects the second moment to zeros. Let $V_f$ be the permutation
representation of $G=\operatorname{Gal}(f)$ on the roots of $f$, and $\Lambda^kV_f$, $\operatorname{Sym}^2V_f$ its
exterior powers and symmetric square. For $p$ not dividing the discriminant of $f$, $\omega_f(p)$ is the
number of fixed points of $\operatorname{Frob}_p$ on the roots, and the Euler factor of $1/\zeta_K(w)$ at $p$ is
$\det(1-\operatorname{Frob}_pp^{-w}\mid V_f)=\sum_k(-1)^k\chi_{\Lambda^kV_f}(\operatorname{Frob}_p)p^{-kw}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:artin}
Write $\chi_2(p)=\chi_{\operatorname{Sym}^2V_f}(\operatorname{Frob}_p)=\tfrac12\omega_f(p)(\omega_f(p)+1)+n_2(p)$, where
$n_2(p)$ is the number of $2$-cycles of $\operatorname{Frob}_p$ on the roots. Then
\[
E_f(s)=\frac{H_f(s)}{L(2s+2,\operatorname{Sym}^2V_f)},
\]
with $H_f(s)$ an Euler product whose factors are $1+O(p^{-2})+O(p^{-2-s})+O(p^{-3-3s})$, absolutely
convergent for $\Re s>-\tfrac23$. For a quadratic $f$ of discriminant $D$, with $\chi_D$ the Kronecker
symbol,
\[
D_f(s)=\frac{\zeta(s+1)\,L(s+1,\chi_D)}{\zeta(2s+2)^2\,L(2s+2,\chi_D)}\;H_f(s),
\]
and extracting the cubic terms as well, $H_f(s)=\zeta(3s+3)L(3s+3,\chi_D)\,\tilde H_f(s)$ with $\tilde H_f$
absolutely convergent for $\Re s>-\tfrac34$. The extraction does not stop: writing $v=p^{-(s+1)}$, the
part of the local factor of $E_f$ at a split prime that survives as $p\to\infty$ with $v$ fixed is
$(1+2v)(1-v)^2=1-3v^2+2v^3$, whose logarithm has infinitely many terms, so that
$\tilde H_f(s)=\zeta(4s+4)^{-1}L(4s+4,\chi_D)^{-2}\,\zeta(5s+5)^3L(5s+5,\chi_D)^3\cdots M_f(s)$ with integer
exponents and $M_f$ absolutely convergent for $\Re s>-1$; $D_f$ is meromorphic in $\Re s>-1$, has a real
pole at $s=-\tfrac23$ (from $\zeta(3s+3)$) and further real poles at $s=-1+1/k$, $k$ odd, and $\Re s=-1$ is
a natural boundary, the zeros of the local factors $F_p$ accumulating at every point of it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The local factor of $D_f$ is $F_p(s)=P_p+p\,b(p)\omega_f(p)p^{-s}$; it contains $p^{-s}$ only to the first
power. Multiplying by $\det(1-\operatorname{Frob}_pp^{-1-s}\mid V_f)=1-\omega p^{-1-s}+\chi_{\Lambda^2}p^{-2-2s}-\cdots$
and using $F_p=1-\omega^2p^{-2}+\omega p^{-1-s}+2\omega^2p^{-2-s}+O(p^{-3})$, the coefficient of $p^{-2-2s}$ in
the $E_f$-factor is $\chi_{\Lambda^2}(\operatorname{Frob}_p)-\omega^2=-\chi_{\operatorname{Sym}^2}(\operatorname{Frob}_p)$, since
$V\otimes V=\Lambda^2V\oplus\operatorname{Sym}^2V$; and $\chi_{\operatorname{Sym}^2}(g)=\tfrac12(\chi_V(g)^2+\chi_V(g^2))$ with
$\chi_V(g^2)=\omega+2n_2$ gives the stated formula. The remaining terms are $-\omega^2p^{-2}$, $2\omega^2p^{-2-s}$,
and terms $p^{-k(1+s)}$, $k\ge3$, with bounded coefficients, whence the convergence statement. For a
quadratic, $V_f=\mathbf 1\oplus\chi_D$, so $\zeta_K=\zeta L(\chi_D)$, $\operatorname{Sym}^2V_f=\mathbf 1\oplus\mathbf 1\oplus\chi_D$,
and the cubic coefficient is $\chi_{\Lambda^3}-\omega\chi_{\Lambda^2}=-\omega\chi_D(p)$ for $\dim V=2$; with
$\omega=1+\chi_D(p)$ this is $-(\chi_D+1)$ up to the sign convention, giving the factor $\zeta(3s+3)L(3s+3,\chi_D)$.
Continuing the expansion of $\log\bigl((1+2v)(1-v)^2\bigr)$ (split $p$) and $\log(1-v^2)$ (inert $p$) and
inverting over the divisors of each degree gives the exponents of the higher factors ($-3v^4$ at split
primes is the first uncancelled term, whence the abscissa $-\tfrac34$); the mixed terms $p^{-a}v^b$,
$a\ge1$, converge absolutely for $\Re s>-1$. In the region $\Re s>-1+1/(K+1)$ the finitely extracted
product vanishes exactly where a local factor $F_p(s)=P_p+p\,b(p)\omega_f(p)p^{-s}$ does, i.e.\ at
$p^{-s}=-P_p/(p\,b(p)\omega_f(p))$; these zeros have real part $\to-1$ and imaginary parts
$(2j+1)\pi/\log p$, dense as $p\to\infty$, which gives the natural boundary. The exponents and the
convergence claims are checked to high precision in the code accompanying~\cite{ReichardtII}.
\end{proof}

The poles of $1/L(2s+2,\operatorname{Sym}^2V_f)$ lie at $s=\rho/2-1$ for the zeros $\rho$ of the symmetric-square
$L$-function; for a quadratic these are the zeros of $\zeta$ (double) and of $L(s,\chi_D)$ (simple). In the
Perron integral for the Riesz means of the diagonal part they produce terms $x^{\rho/2}$, exactly as the
zeros of $\zeta$ do through $1/\zeta(2s)$ in the work of Goldston and
Suriajaya~\cite{GoldstonSuriajaya2021} on $\sum_{h\le x}\Sig(h)$; see Section~\ref{sec:riesz}.

For quadratics the pair singular series itself has a closed form of twin-prime type.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quadratic}
Let $f=at^2+bt+c\in\Z[t]$ be irreducible with discriminant $D=b^2-4ac$, and let $Q(h)=a^2h^2-D$. For
every prime $p\nmid 2aD$: $\omega_f(p)=1+\chi_D(p)$, the two roots of $f$ modulo a split prime differ by
$\pm\sqrt D/a$, and hence $\nu_f(p,h)=\omega_f(p)$ if $p\mid h$, $\nu_f(p,h)=1$ if $p\mid Q(h)$ and $p\nmid h$,
and $\nu_f(p,h)=0$ otherwise. Consequently
\[
S_f(h)=C(f)^2\prod_pP_p\;\prod_{p\mid h}\frac{p-\omega_f(p)}{p-2\omega_f(p)}\;\prod_{\substack{p\mid Q(h)\\p\nmid h}}\frac{p-3}{p-4},
\]
with the finitely many primes dividing $2aD$ carrying their exact local factors instead. For $f=t^2+1$:
$S_f(h)=0$ for odd $h$ and, for even $h$,
$S_f(h)=2C(f)^2\prod_{p\equiv1(4)}P_p\prod_{p\mid h,\,p\equiv1(4)}\frac{p-2}{p-4}\prod_{p\mid h^2+4,\,p\nmid h}\frac{p-3}{p-4}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $p\nmid 2aD$ split, the roots are $(-b\pm\sqrt D)/2a$, so the ordered pairs of distinct roots have
differences $\pm\sqrt D/a$, and $h\equiv\pm\sqrt D/a$ iff $p\mid a^2h^2-D$; for $p$ odd exactly one of the two
signs occurs, so $\nu_f(p,h)=1$. Then $1+g_p(h)$ equals $1/(1-\omega/p)$ for $p\mid h$, $(1-3/p)/(1-2/p)^2$
for $p\mid Q(h)$, $p\nmid h$ (where $\omega=2$), and $P_p$ otherwise; dividing by $P_p$ gives the factors
$(p-\omega)/(p-2\omega)$ and $(p-3)/(p-4)$. For $t^2+1$, $\omega(2)=1$ makes $P_2=0$ and the local factor at
$2$ equal to $2$ for even $h$ and $0$ for odd $h$.
\end{proof}

The factor $(p-\omega)/(p-2\omega)$ for $p\mid h$ is the analogue of the twin-prime factor $(p-1)/(p-2)$;
the new ingredient is the second product over the primes represented by the quadratic form $Q(h)$. This
makes $S_f(h)$ computable exactly, by sieving $h$ and $Q(h)$, for all $h$ up to $4\cdot10^7$ in a minute.

\subsection{Theorems}\label{sec:thms}

The fractional-part sum $\sum_d a_f(d)\{H/d\}$ equals $\frac1{2C}\log H+O(1)$ if the fractional parts
$\{H/d\}$ have mean $\tfrac12$ against the weights $a_f(d)$; for $f(t)=t$ this is the theorem of
Friedlander--Goldston and Vaughan~\cite{FriedlanderGoldston1995,Vaughan2001}
($\sum_{h\le H}\Sig(h)=H-\tfrac12\log H+O((\log H)^{2/3})$), whose proof needs exponential-sum
estimates of Vinogradov type. Under a Ces\`aro weight the fractional parts are tamed and an elementary
argument suffices.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:smoothed}
Let $\phi(\theta)=\theta-\theta^2$. For $H\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothed}
\sum_{h\le H}\Bigl(1-\frac hH\Bigr)\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)=-\tfrac12\,C(f)\log H+O_f(1)+\frac{C(f)^2}{H}\,\mathrm{Off}^{\ast}_f(H),
\end{equation}
where, with $m=s'-s\bmod d\in[1,d-1]$ and the sum over unordered pairs of distinct roots,
\[
\mathrm{Off}^{\ast}_f(H)=\sum_{d\ge2}W_f(d)\sum_{\{s,s'\}}\Bigl[-\frac{m(d-m)}d+\frac d2\Bigl(\phi\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac{H-m}d\Bigr\}\Bigr)+\phi\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac{H+m}d\Bigr\}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr]
\]
converges absolutely. In particular:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] if $\omega_f(p)\le1$ for all $p$, e.g.\ for every linear $f$, then $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f\equiv0$ and
$\sum_{h\le H}(1-h/H)(S_f(h)-C^2)=-\tfrac12C\log H+O_f(1)$;
\item[(ii)] for general $f$, Conjecture~\ref{conj} in Ces\`aro form holds, with the leading term
$-\tfrac12C(f)\log H$, if and only if $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)=o(H\log H)$; trivially
$\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)\ll H(\log H)^{r_f-1}$, where $r_f\ge2$ is the number of orbits of the Galois
group of $f$ on ordered pairs of roots.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Multiply~\eqref{eq:expansion} by $H-h$ and sum. As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion},
\[
\sum_{h\le H}(H-h)\bigl(S_f(h)-C^2\bigr)=C^2\sum_{d\ge2}W_f(d)\sum_{s,s'\bmod d}B_d(s'-s),\qquad
B_d(m)=\sum_{\substack{h\le H\\h\equiv m\ (d)}}(H-h)-\frac{H^2}{2d},
\]
the subtraction of $H^2/(2d)$ being allowed because $\sum_{d\mid q}\mu(q/d)=0$ for $q>1$. An elementary
computation gives, for $1\le m\le d$ and every $d\ge1$ (also for $d>H$),
\[
B_d(m)=-\frac{Hm}d+\frac{m^2}{2d}+\frac H2-\frac m2+\frac d2\,\phi\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac{H-m}d\Bigr\}\Bigr),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
B_d(d)=-\frac H2+\frac d2\,\phi\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac Hd\Bigr\}\Bigr),
\]
and $B_d(m)+B_d(d-m)$ is the bracket in the definition of $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f$. The diagonal is
\[
\sum_d a_f(d)B_d(d)=-\frac H2\sum_{d\le H}a_f(d)+\frac12\sum_{d\le H}d\,a_f(d)\,\phi\Bigl(\Bigl\{\frac Hd\Bigr\}\Bigr)-\frac{H^2}2\sum_{d>H}\frac{a_f(d)}d,
\]
using $B_d(d)=-H^2/(2d)$ for $d>H$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet} the first term is
$-\frac{H}{2C}\log H+O(H)$ and, by partial summation, $\sum_{d>H}a_f(d)/d=1/(CH)+o(1/H)$, so the third
term is $O(H)$. In the second, $0\le\phi\le\tfrac14$ and $\sum_{d\le H}d\,|a_f(d)|=O(H)$ by Shiu's
theorem~\cite{Shiu1980} for the non-negative multiplicative function $d\,|a_f(d)|$, whose value at $p$
is $\omega_f(p)(1+O(1/p))$ and satisfies $\sum_{p\le x}(\omega_f(p)-1)/p=O(1)$. Dividing by $H$
gives~\eqref{eq:smoothed}. Absolute convergence of $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f$ and the bound in~(ii) follow
from the bounds $|B_d(m)+B_d(d-m)|\le d/4$ for $d\le H$ (the terms linear in $m$ cancel in the bracket;
individual values $B_d(m)$ can be of size $H$) and $\ll d+H^2/d$ for $d>H$, together with
$\sum_{d\le x}|W_f(d)|\,\omega_f(d)^2\,d\ll x(\log x)^{r_f-1}$ (Shiu's theorem again, since the mean
value of $\omega_f(p)^2$ over primes is $r_f$ by Chebotarev) and the finiteness of
$\sum_d W_f(d)\sum_{h\le H}\nu_f(d,h)(H-h)$. If $\omega_f(p)\le1$ for all $p$ there are no pairs of
distinct roots and $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f=0$.
\end{proof}

For $f(t)=t$, Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed}(i) is the Ces\`aro form of the Friedlander--Goldston--Vaughan
asymptotic, with an elementary proof of the leading term; Goldston and
Suriajaya~\cite{GoldstonSuriajaya2021,GoldstonSuriajaya2021b} have shown that the error term of the
Riesz means of $\sum_{h\le x}\Sig(h)$ is an explicit formula over the zeros of $\zeta$. In our setting the
generating series is $\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$, and the same analysis would express the fluctuations of
$\sum_{h\le H}S_f(h)$ through the zeros of the Dedekind zeta function of $K$; we return to this in
Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:two}
Part~(ii) deserves emphasis. The Galois group of $f$ acts on ordered pairs of distinct roots; it is
$2$-transitive exactly when $r_f=2$, which is the generic case ($S_n$, $A_n$, and every $2$-transitive
group). Then the trivial bound is $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)\ll H\log H$, and the leading term
$-\tfrac12C(f)\log H$ of Conjecture~\ref{conj} in Ces\`aro form follows from \emph{any} saving of an
unbounded factor over the trivial bound: one needs the residue classes $s'-s\bmod d$ of differences of
distinct roots to be distributed among the $d-1$ nonzero classes with a discrepancy that is $o(1)$ on
average over $d\le H$ against the weights $W_f(d)$, not a power saving. For a quadratic the pairs are
$(\pm\sqrt D)$ and the differences $\pm2\sqrt D\bmod d$; the equidistribution of the roots of
$x^2\equiv D\pmod d$ on average over $d$, with a power saving, is Hooley's theorem~\cite{Hooley1963},
and the analogous statements for prime moduli are those of Duke, Friedlander and
Iwaniec~\cite{DFI1995} and T\'oth~\cite{Toth2000}. What is not in the literature is the average with
the multiplicative weights $W_f(d)$ and the fractional-part test function; part~III of this
series~\cite{ReichardtIII} takes this apart and proves what can be proved unconditionally, and we do not claim it here. For $r_f\ge3$ the trivial bound has an extra power of
$\log H$ and a genuine cancellation among the orbits is required (Section~\ref{sec:galois}).
\end{remark}

The full conjecture~\eqref{eq:conj}, with a constant term, needs more than the leading term:

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Hypothesis (E).} $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)=O(H)$. In words: the fractional parts $\{(H\pm m)/d\}$, $m$ ranging
over the differences of distinct roots of $f$ modulo $d$, have a mean against the weights $W_f(d)$ that differs from
$\tfrac12$ by $O(1/H)$ on average. The sharp remainder $\mathrm{Off}_f(H)$ of~\eqref{eq:split}, for which
$\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)=\sum_{t<H}\mathrm{Off}_f(t)-\tfrac12(1-D_f(1))H$ with $D_f(1)=\sum_{d\ge1}a_f(d)/d$~\cite{ReichardtIII},
is \emph{not} $O(1)$, although earlier versions of this paper offered that as an equivalent sharp form: its increments
$\mathrm{Off}_f(H)-\mathrm{Off}_f(H-1)$ are the off-diagonal part of $(S_f(H)-C^2)/C^2$, which for $t^2+1$ is unbounded
along the $h$ constructed in Remark~\ref{rem:sharp} (there the diagonal part is $O(1)$ and the growth is entirely
off-diagonal). Hypothesis~(E) says that the Ces\`aro mean of $\mathrm{Off}_f$ is bounded, nothing more; the sharp
$\mathrm{Off}_f(H)$ is bounded by $1.3$ to $10^7$ only because $\log\log10^7<3$.

\medskip\noindent
This is the equidistribution statement of Remark~\ref{rem:two} with a bounded, rather than merely
$o(H\log H)$, error; we have not proved it. Part~III~\cite{ReichardtIII} shows that $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)$ is not $o(H)$: the moduli
$d$ with a large agreeing part of the two roots contribute an explicit main term $c_{\mathrm{off}}(f)H$ with
$c_{\mathrm{off}}(f)<0$, so that the constant $A_f$ of~\eqref{eq:conj} is the diagonal constant plus $C(f)^2c_{\mathrm{off}}(f)$;
for $t^2+1$, $c_{\mathrm{off}}=-0.1344$, and the mean of $\mathrm{Off}_f(t)$ is predicted to be $-0.0098$, in agreement with
Table~\ref{tab:off} and with exact data to $10^7$. We test it directly for quadratics, where $\omega_f(p)=1+\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{disc}f}{p}\bigr)$
allows the diagonal sum $\sum_d a_f(d)\{H/d\}$ to be computed exactly to $d\le\offDmax$, so that
$\mathrm{Off}_f(H)$ is obtained from~\eqref{eq:split} by subtraction (Section~\ref{sec:sumnum}).

statistical error but genuine arithmetic fluctuation of $\Sigma_f(H)$ (the terms $S_f(h)-C^2$ are large
and of both signs, and the partial sums over one decade of $H$ do not average them completely); it is
largest for polynomials with large $C$, which have no roots modulo the small primes and hence the most
irregular $S_f$. The constant $A_f/C$ is negative and of order $-1$ for most polynomials, i.e.\ the
sum is more negative at finite $H$ than $-\tfrac12C\log H$; this is the quantity that governs the
finite-$T$ variance in~\eqref{eq:law}. Because the mean $C$ of this sample is close to $1$,
Table~\ref{tab:sums} cannot by itself separate $C$ from $C^2$; that is the purpose of the next test.

\paragraph{The exact test: $C$ against $C^2$.} Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic} removes the Euler-product
truncation of the $h$-dependent part for quadratics: $S_f(h)/C^2$ is $\prod_pP_p$ (an absolutely
convergent product, terms $1+O(p^{-2})$, which we truncate at $p\le P=2\cdot10^6$ and complete by its
prime-number-theorem tail, $\prod_{p>P}P_p\approx\exp(-2E_1(\log P))\approx1-7\cdot10^{-8}$, each split prime
contributing $\log P_p\approx-4/p^2$; the residual error is below $10^{-10}$) times a finite product over
the primes dividing $h$ and $Q(h)=a^2h^2-D$, which we
obtain by sieving $h\le10^6$ and $Q(h)$ with the roots $\pm\sqrt D/a$ of $Q$ modulo each prime up to
$\sqrt{Q(10^6)}$; the remaining cofactor is $1$ or a prime. $C(f)$ is computed from $L(1,\chi_{D_0})$,
$D_0$ the fundamental discriminant, by the digamma formula, times an absolutely convergent correction.
We checked the implementation against the definition~\eqref{eq:S} for four quadratics and $h\le3000$,
truncating both at the same set of primes: the two agree to $\exRatioSpread$ in relative terms, i.e.\ to
rounding, including the vanishing values. The tail correction matters: a relative error $\delta$ in
$\prod_pP_p$ enters $\Sigma_f(H)$ as a drift $\delta C^2H$, i.e.\ $0.07C^2$ at $H=10^6$ for $P=2\cdot10^6$
without it, which biases the fitted exponent towards $2$ (without the correction the sharp-sum fit below
gives $\alpha=0.81$, $0.87$ and $0.91$ at $P=10^6$, $2\cdot10^6$ and $10^7$; with it, the $P=2\cdot10^6$ and $P=10^7$
values agree). We then chose $\exN$ irreducible quadratics without fixed divisor, $a\le7$, with $C(f)$ spread
uniformly in $\log C$ over $[\exCmin,\exCmax]$, a lever arm of more than a decade in $C$, and fitted
\[
\frac{\Sigma^\ast_f(10^6)-\Sigma^\ast_f(10^3)}{\log10^3}=-k\,C(f)^\alpha,\qquad
\Sigma^\ast_f(H)=\sum_{h\le H}\Bigl(1-\frac hH\Bigr)\bigl(S_f(h)-C^2\bigr),
\]
in two ways (Table~\ref{tab:exact}, Figure~\ref{fig:exact}): by least squares on
$\log(-\text{slope})$ against $\log C$, which weights every polynomial equally in relative terms, and by
unweighted least squares on the slopes themselves, which weights the large-$C$ polynomials more. The
results are
\[
\alpha=\exCesAlpha\pm\exCesAlphaSe,\ k=\exCesK\quad\text{(logarithmic)};\qquad
\alpha=\exCesAlphaLin\pm\exCesAlphaLinSe,\ k=\exCesKLin\pm\exCesKLinSe\quad\text{(unweighted)},
\]
against the prediction $\alpha=1$, $k=\tfrac12$ of Conjecture~\ref{conj}, and more than $\exCesSigmaTwoMin$ standard
errors from $\alpha=2$; the correlation of slope$/C$ with $C$ is $\exCesCorrC$, that of slope$/C^2$ with $C$ is
$\exCesCorrCC$. The mean slope$/C$ is $\exCesSlopeC\pm\exCesSlopeCsd$ over the $\exN$ polynomials, and by decades
$\exCesDecA\pm\exCesDecAsd$, $\exCesDecB\pm\exCesDecBsd$, $\exCesDecC\pm\exCesDecCsd$ over $10^3$--$10^4$, $10^4$--$10^5$,
$10^5$--$10^6$: the Ces\`aro sum follows $-\tfrac12C\log H$ to three decimal places, for every polynomial and in every
decade, exactly as Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed} predicts for the diagonal once $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f(H)/H$ is bounded.

The same fit for the sharp sum $\Sigma_f(H)$ gives $\alpha=\exAlpha\pm\exAlphaSe$ (logarithmic) and
$\exAlphaLin\pm\exAlphaLinSe$ (unweighted), mean slope$/C$ $\exSlopeC\pm\exSlopeCsd$, and by decades $\exDecA$, $\exDecB$,
$\exDecC$ with scatter $\pm\exDecAsd$, $\pm\exDecBsd$, $\pm\exDecCsd$; the $C^2$ law is excluded there too, but the scatter is
forty times larger. The difference is the oscillation of $\Sigma_f(H)$ in $H$: the sharp sum evaluated
at the two endpoints $10^3$ and $10^6$ carries a remainder of size about $1$ that does not average out (and that is
not even bounded as $H\to\infty$, Remark~\ref{rem:sharp}), which is why Conjecture~\ref{conj} is stated for the
Ces\`aro mean only, and it is what an earlier version of this paper misread
as a coefficient ``systematically short of $\tfrac12$''. The Ces\`aro sum is the quantity the exact identity
controls, and it should be the quantity one measures.

\paragraph{The off-diagonal remainder is bounded (Hypothesis~(E) for quadratics).} For $\offN$

(the tail is about $H/(C\cdot\offDmax)$), and $\mathrm{Off}_f(H)$ as the difference

bounded oscillating quantity that Hypothesis~(E) asserts.

\subsection{The function-field analogue: the off-diagonal vanishes for small moduli}\label{sec:ff}

Over $\A=\F_q[u]$, $q$ odd, every object above has a verbatim analogue: for $f\in\A[t]$ irreducible in
$t$, separable and without fixed prime divisor, $|P|=q^{\deg P}$ replaces $p$, ``$h\le H$'' becomes
``$h\ne0$, $\deg h<N$'' ($q^N-1$ elements) and $\log H$ becomes $N$; the products $C(f)$, $b$, $P_P$,
$W_f$, $a_f$ converge absolutely as before. The identity~\eqref{eq:identity} holds with one decisive
simplification.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ff}
Let $f\in\A[t]$ be as above, let $a_f(d)=W_f(d)\,\omega_f(d)$ with $W_f$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion}
(products over the primes of $\A$), and put $P(1)=\prod_PP_P=W_f(1)$. For every $N\ge1$,
\begin{align*}
\sum_{\substack{h\ne0\\\deg h<N}}\Bigl(\frac{S_f(h)}{C(f)^2}-1\Bigr)
&=-\sum_{1\le\deg d\le N}a_f(d)\;-\;q^N\sum_{\deg d>N}\frac{a_f(d)}{|d|}\;+\;\bigl(1-P(1)\bigr)\;+\;\mathrm{Off}_f(N),\\
\mathrm{Off}_f(N)&=\sum_{\deg d>N}W_f(d)\sum_{\substack{s,s'\bmod d\\ s\ne s'}}\Bigl(\mathbf 1_{0\le\deg\tilde m<N}-q^{N-\deg d}\Bigr),
\qquad \tilde m\equiv s'-s\ (d),\ \deg\tilde m<\deg d,
\end{align*}
where $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$
runs over squarefree $d$ of degree $>N$ only. In particular the pairs of \emph{distinct} roots modulo $d$
contribute nothing for $\deg d\le N$: the off-diagonal part of~\eqref{eq:split} vanishes identically for
all moduli of degree $\le N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The expansion $S_f(h)/C^2=\sum_Qb(Q)c^f_Q(h)$ over squarefree monic $Q$ is proved as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion}, and for $Q\ne1$
\[
\sum_{\substack{h\ne0\\\deg h<N}}c^f_Q(h)=\sum_{d\mid Q}|d|\,\mu(Q/d)\,\omega_f(Q/d)^2\sum_{s,s'\bmod d}\mathrm{cnt}_d(s'-s),
\]
where $\mathrm{cnt}_d(m)=\#\{h\ne0:\ \deg h<N,\ h\equiv m\ (d)\}$.
Since $\sum_{d\mid Q}\mu(Q/d)=0$ we may replace $\mathrm{cnt}_d$ by $\psi_d(m)=\mathrm{cnt}_d(m)-q^{N-\deg d}$.
For $\deg d\le N$ every residue class modulo $d$ contains exactly $q^{N-\deg d}$ polynomials of degree $<N$
(the class of $m$ is $\{\tilde m+dg:\ \deg g<N-\deg d\}$), of which one is $0$ iff $d\mid m$; hence
$\psi_d(m)=-\mathbf 1_{d\mid m}$, including $d=1$, where $\psi_1=-1$ because $h=0$ is excluded. For
$\deg d>N$ a class contains at most one nonzero $h$ of degree $<N$, namely $\tilde m$ when
$0\le\deg\tilde m<N$, so $\psi_d(m)=\mathbf 1_{0\le\deg\tilde m<N}-q^{N-\deg d}$. Regrouping the moduli
$Q=dm$, $(m,d)=1$, by $d$ (absolute convergence as in Theorem~\ref{thm:expansion}) gives
$\sum_{Q\ne1}b(Q)\sum_hc^f_Q(h)=\sum_dW_f(d)\sum_{s,s'}\psi_d(s'-s)+1$, the $+1$ compensating the term
$Q=1$, i.e.\ $(d,m)=(1,1)$, of the regrouped sum, whose value is $\psi_1=-1$. For $\deg d\le N$,
$\sum_{s,s'}\psi_d(s'-s)=-\#\{(s,s'):\ s\equiv s'\ (d)\}=-\omega_f(d)$, which gives $-P(1)-\sum_{1\le\deg d\le N}a_f(d)$.
For $\deg d>N$ the pairs with $s=s'$ have $\tilde m=0$ and contribute $-\omega_f(d)q^{N-\deg d}$, the
pairs with $s\ne s'$ contribute $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$.
\end{proof}

Over $\Z$ the modulus $d=1$ drops out of~\eqref{eq:identity} because $\#\{h\le H\}=H$ exactly; over $\A$
it leaves the constant $1-P(1)$ because $h=0$ is excluded from the $q^N$ polynomials of degree $<N$.
The three diagonal terms are the exact analogue of $-C^2\sum_da_f(d)\{H/d\}$ in~\eqref{eq:split}: the
fractional part $\{H/d\}$, of mean $\tfrac12$ for $d\le H$ and equal to $H/d$ for $d>H$, is replaced by
$1$ for $\deg d\le N$ and by $q^N/|d|$ for $\deg d>N$.

The counterpart of Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet} is the following. Put $T=q^{-s}$. The Dirichlet series
$D_f(s)=\sum_da_f(d)|d|^{-s}$ is a power series in $T$ whose coefficient of $T^j$ is $\sum_{\deg d=j}a_f(d)$,
and the local computation of Theorems~\ref{thm:dirichlet} and~\ref{thm:artin} goes through verbatim:
$D_f(s)=\zeta_\A(s+1)L(s+1,\chi_D)E_f(s)$ with $\zeta_\A(s+1)=1/(1-T)$, $L(s+1,\chi_D)$ a polynomial in
$T$, and $E_f(s)=\tilde H_f(s)(1-q^{-1}T^2)^2/L(2s+2,\chi_D)$, where $\tilde H_f$ converges absolutely for
$|T|<q$ and the polynomial $L(2s+2,\chi_D)$ has its zeros on $|T|=q^{3/4}$ by Weil's theorem. Hence
$D_f$ is meromorphic in $|T|<q^{3/4}$ with a single simple pole at $T=1$, and $(1-T)D_f\to1/C(f)$ as
$T\to1$ by the same computation as over $\Q$; therefore
$\sum_{\deg d=j}a_f(d)=1/C(f)+O(q^{-(3/4-\varepsilon)j})$,
$\sum_{1\le\deg d\le N}a_f(d)=N/C(f)+c_f+O(q^{-(3/4-\varepsilon)N})$ and
$q^N\sum_{\deg d>N}a_f(d)/|d|=1/(C(f)(q-1))+O(q^{-(3/4-\varepsilon)N})$. Hence over $\F_q[u]$ the
analogue of Conjecture~\ref{conj},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ffconj}
\sum_{\substack{h\ne0\\\deg h<N}}\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)=-C(f)\,N+O_f(1),
\end{equation}
is \emph{equivalent} to $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)=O(1)$: the whole content of Hypothesis~(E) sits in the
off-diagonal pairs modulo the moduli of degree $>N$. (The coefficient is $-C$ rather than $-\tfrac12C$
because the mean $\tfrac12$ of $\{H/d\}$ is replaced by the exact value $1$.) The remainder is more
concrete than it looks: its first part is a finite sum.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:support}
Let $f=t^2-D$ with $D\in\A$ not a square, and for squarefree $d$ let $A_N(d)$ be the number of ordered
pairs $(s,s')$ of roots of $f$ modulo $d$ with $s\ne s'$ whose difference $\tilde m$ is nonzero of degree
$<N$. Then
\[
\mathrm{Off}_f(N)=\sum_{N<\deg d\le M(N)}W_f(d)\,A_N(d)\;-\;q^N\sum_{\deg d>N}\frac{W_f(d)\bigl(\omega_f(d)^2-\omega_f(d)\bigr)}{|d|},
\]
with $M(N)=N-1+\max(2N-2,\deg D)$.
The series converges absolutely, and it is computable from the exact identities
\[
\sum_{d}\frac{W_f(d)\,\omega_f(d)^2}{|d|}=1,\qquad
\sum_{d}\frac{W_f(d)\,\omega_f(d)}{|d|}=\prod_P\Bigl(1+\frac{\omega_f(P)(1-\omega_f(P))}{(|P|-\omega_f(P))^2}\Bigr)=:A_f(1),
\]
both sums over all squarefree monic $d$ including $d=1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By definition $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)=\sum_{\deg d>N}W_f(d)\bigl(A_N(d)-(\omega_f(d)^2-\omega_f(d))q^{N-\deg d}\bigr)$,
so it remains to bound the degrees with $A_N(d)\ne0$. Let $(s,s')$, $s\ne s'$, have $0\le\deg\tilde m<N$,
and split $d=d_1d_2$ with $d_2=\prod\{P\mid d:\ s\equiv s'\ (P)\}$. Then $d_2\mid\tilde m$, so
$\deg d_2\le\deg\tilde m\le N-1$ because $\tilde m\ne0$. For $P\mid d_1$ the two roots are distinct modulo
$P$, hence $P\nmid D$ and $s'-s\equiv\pm2\sqrt D$, so $\tilde m^2\equiv4D\pmod{d_1}$ and
$d_1\mid\tilde m^2-4D$; since $D$ is not a square, $\tilde m^2-4D\ne0$ and
$\deg d_1\le\deg(\tilde m^2-4D)\le\max(2N-2,\deg D)$. Adding the two bounds gives $\deg d\le M(N)$. For
the identities, $W_f(d)/|d|=b(d)\sum_{(m,d)=1}\mu(m)b(m)\omega_f(m)^2$, so
$\sum_dW_f(d)\omega_f(d)^2/|d|=\sum_Qb(Q)\omega_f(Q)^2\sum_{d\mid Q}\mu(Q/d)=b(1)\omega_f(1)^2=1$, and
$\sum_dW_f(d)\omega_f(d)/|d|=\sum_Qb(Q)\sum_{d\mid Q}\mu(Q/d)\omega_f(d)\omega_f(Q/d)^2$ is multiplicative
in $Q$ with local factor $1+b_P(\omega_f(P)-\omega_f(P)^2)$; all rearrangements are justified by absolute
convergence, since $\sum_Qb(Q)\omega_f(Q)^2<\infty$.
\end{proof}

So~\eqref{eq:ffconj} is equivalent to the statement that, over the moduli $d$ of degree between $N$ and
$M(N)\approx3N$, the differences of distinct roots of $f$ modulo $d$ fall among the residues of degree
$<N$ with the frequency $q^{N-\deg d}$, up to a bounded total discrepancy; nothing about moduli of degree
$\le N$ or $>M(N)$ is involved. Neither term of Lemma~\ref{lem:support} is bounded a priori; only their
difference is claimed to be. For a prime modulus $P$ of degree $k$ the count asks whether the residue
$2\sqrt D\bmod P$ has degree $<N$; written through the additive characters trivial on $\A_{<N}$, this is
a Weyl sum of the roots of a quadratic congruence over primes of fixed degree, the function-field
analogue of the sums of Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec, and for fixed $N$ and $q\to\infty$ each of the
finitely many degrees in Lemma~\ref{lem:support} is an algebraic count with square-root cancellation by
Deligne's theorem~\cite{Katz1988}, as in the work of Bary-Soroker and Entin on prime values of
polynomials over $\F_q[u]$~\cite{BarySoroker2012,Entin2016}. We have not written this out; the large-$q$
form of \eqref{eq:ffconj}, with $O(q^{-1/2})$ in place of $O(1)$, is where we expect the first theorem
beyond the linear case, and Lemma~\ref{lem:support} says exactly which finite family of counts it
requires.

\paragraph{Exact computation.} Theorem~\ref{thm:ff} and Lemma~\ref{lem:support} can be checked exactly,
and we did so in two independent ways. First (\texttt{research/experiments/ff.ts}), for each nonzero $h$ of degree $<N$,
$S_f(h)/(C^2P(1))$ is a finite product over the primes dividing $h(h^2-4D)$; the two diagonal sums are
computed from the local data (the series through $A_f(1)$), and $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$ follows from the
identity by subtraction. Second (\texttt{research/experiments/ff-tail.ts}), $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$ is computed from
Lemma~\ref{lem:support}: the finite sum from the roots of $f$ modulo every squarefree $d$ of degree
$\le M(N)$ with all prime factors split or ramified, assembled by the Chinese remainder theorem, and the
series from the two identities; for the $\fftailBruteN$ smallest cases the finite sum was recomputed by
brute force (all monic $d$, roots by exhaustive search), with agreement to $\fftailBruteDev$.
Tables~\ref{tab:ff} and~\ref{tab:fftail} report $f=t^2-D$ for $q=3,5,7$ and $\ffCases$ choices of $D$.
The two computations of $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$ agree to $\fftailMaxDev$ (the residual is the truncation of
the Euler products at the prime table); the increments of $\sum_{\deg d\le N}a_f(d)$ converge to
$1/C(f)$ and the diagonal tail to $1/(C(f)(q-1))$ as predicted (after a transient at $N\le2$ for $q=3$,
where a prime of norm $3$ with $\omega_f=2$ makes $P_P=-3$ and $P(1)<0$); and $\mathrm{Off}_f(N)$ is
bounded, $|\mathrm{Off}_f(N)|\le\ffOffMax$ in all cases with a variation over $N$ of at most
$\ffOffSwing$, with no sign of growth.

\section{Where the zeros of $L(s,\chi_D)$ enter, and why they are hard to see}\label{sec:riesz}

\emph{This section is about lower-order terms. It explains the mechanism by which the zeros of $\zeta$
and of $L(s,\chi_D)$ enter the fluctuations of $\sum_{h\le H}S_f(h)$, exactly as the zeros of $\zeta$ enter
the twin-prime case, and reports an honest negative result: at the ranges we can compute they are not
yet visible.}

\subsection{The explicit formula for the diagonal}

For $f(t)=t$ the mechanism is known: Vaughan~\cite{Vaughan2001} proved
$\sum_{k\le x}(x-k)\Sig(k)=\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac12x\log x+\tfrac12(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)x+E(x)$ with
$E(x)=\Omega_\pm(x^{1/4})$, and Goldston and Suriajaya~\cite{GoldstonSuriajaya2021} showed that for the
Riesz means $\sum_{k\le x}(x-k)^m\Sig(k)$, $m\ge2$, the error term is
$x^{m-1}\sum_{|\gamma|\le U}a(\rho)x^{\rho/2}+O(x^{m-1+\varepsilon})$ over the zeros $\rho=\beta+i\gamma$ of
$\zeta$, the zeros entering through the factor $1/\zeta(2s)$ of the generating Dirichlet series, and that
unconditionally $E_m(x)=\Omega_\pm(x^{m-3/4})$. Theorem~\ref{thm:artin} puts the diagonal part of our
sum in exactly this situation. Let $A_f=a_f*1$, i.e.\ $A_f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}a_f(d)$, so that the diagonal
part of $\sum_{h\le H}(S_f(h)-C^2)$ is $-C^2\sum_da_f(d)\{H/d\}=C^2\bigl(\sum_{n\le H}A_f(n)-H\sum_da_f(d)/d\bigr)$
and $\sum_nA_f(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)D_f(s)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:explicit}
Let $f$ be an irreducible quadratic with discriminant $D$, $m\ge2$, and assume the Riemann hypothesis for
$\zeta$ and for $L(s,\chi_D)$. Then there is $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that
\[
\sum_{n\le x}(x-n)^mA_f(n)=P_m(x)+c_f\,x^{m-2/3}+x^{m-1}\sum_{\substack{\rho\\|\gamma|\le U}}a_m(\rho)\,x^{\rho/2}+O(x^{m-3/4-\varepsilon_0}),
\]
for some $x^5\le U\le2x^5$, where $P_m$ collects the residues at $s=1$ ($x^{m+1}$) and $s=0$
($x^m\log x$ and $x^m$), $c_f\,x^{m-2/3}$ is the residue at the pole $s=-\tfrac23$ of $\zeta(3s+3)$,
$\rho$ runs over the zeros of $\zeta(s)^2L(s,\chi_D)$, and $a_m(\rho)$ is the residue of
\[
\frac{m!\,\zeta(s)\zeta(s+1)L(s+1,\chi_D)\zeta(3s+3)L(3s+3,\chi_D)\tilde H_f(s)}{\zeta(2s+2)^2L(2s+2,\chi_D)\,s(s+1)\cdots(s+m)}
\]
at $s=\rho/2-1$ (a polynomial of degree one in $\log x$ at the double zeros of $\zeta$).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:artin} the generating series $\zeta(s)D_f(s)$ of $A_f$ is meromorphic in $\Re s>-1$;
in the strip $-\tfrac34-\varepsilon_0\le\Re s\le2$, for $\varepsilon_0<\tfrac1{20}$, its poles are exactly
$s=1$, $s=0$, $s=-\tfrac23$ and the points $s=\rho/2-1$ (the next real pole is at $-\tfrac45$ and the next
family of zero-induced poles, from $\zeta(4s+4)L(4s+4,\chi_D)^2$, lies on $\Re s=-\tfrac78$). The proof of
\cite[Theorem~1]{GoldstonSuriajaya2021} applies with $\zeta(2s+2)^2L(2s+2,\chi_D)$ in place of
$\zeta(2s)$: the contour is moved to $\Re s=-\tfrac34-\varepsilon_0$ at a height $U$ chosen, by a
zero-density estimate for $\zeta$ and $L(s,\chi_D)$, so that $1/(\zeta^2L)$ is not large on the horizontal
segments, and the Riesz weight of order $m\ge2$ makes the vertical integral absolutely convergent; on the
vertical line, $\Re(2s+2)=\tfrac12-2\varepsilon_0$ lies just left of the critical line, and the Riemann
hypothesis with the functional equation gives $1/\zeta(2s+2)$, $1/L(2s+2,\chi_D)\ll|t|^{2\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon}$
there, while $\tilde H_f$ is bounded. Unlike the linear case, the contour cannot be moved across the
whole critical strip of $\zeta(2s+2)$, because $\Re s=-1$ is a natural boundary; this is why the
statement is conditional and why the error term is $x^{m-3/4-\varepsilon_0}$ rather than $x^{m-1+\varepsilon}$.
(Moving the contour further, to $\Re s=-\tfrac78-\varepsilon_0$, is possible but useless: $|D_f|$ grows
towards the natural boundary and the coefficients of the second family of zero terms are individually
enormous, cancelling against the line integral.) We state this as a proposition rather than a theorem
because we have transplanted the argument and checked the new ingredients (the location of the poles,
the growth of $\tilde H_f$, the exponents), but have not written out the contour estimates line by line.
\end{proof}

Since $A_f(n)\ge0$ for $f=t^2+1$, Landau's theorem applies as in \cite[Theorem~4]{GoldstonSuriajaya2021} and
gives, unconditionally, $\Omega_\pm(x^{m-3/4})$ for the error term after $P_m(x)+c_fx^{m-2/3}$ (the pole at
$-\tfrac23$ is real and must be subtracted first; the first non-real singularity is at $-\tfrac34+3.01i$,
from the zero $\tfrac12+6.02i$ of $L(s,\chi_{-4})$). Thus the fluctuations of the diagonal
part of the pair-correlation sum of $t^2+1$ are governed by the zeros of $\zeta$ and of $L(s,\chi_{-4})$,
the Dirichlet $L$-function of $\Q(i)$: this is the precise sense in which the field cut out by a
polynomial enters the second moment of its prime values. Under the Riemann Hypothesis for $\zeta$ and
$L(s,\chi_{-4})$ the diagonal error after the smooth terms is $O(x^{m-3/4+\varepsilon})$; a zero of real
part $\beta>\tfrac12$ would produce a term of size $x^{m-1+\beta/2}$.

\subsection{A numerical search}

The proposition invites a test: compute the Riesz means, remove the main terms, and look in the residual for
oscillations $\cos(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\log x)$. We did this for $f=t^2+1$ in two versions
(\texttt{research/experiments/riesz.ts}, \texttt{riesz-analysis.ts}, \texttt{riesz-diag.ts}, \texttt{riesz-fit.ts}).

\emph{Full sum.} By Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic}, $S_f(h)$ is computable exactly by sieving $h$ and
$h^2+4$; we did so for all $h\le\rieszX$, formed $R_m(x)=\sum_{h\le x}(x-h)^mS_f(h)$ at $4096$ points
equally spaced in $u=\log x\in[\log10^4,\log\rieszX]$, removed the smooth terms by least squares and
analysed the residual, normalised by $x^{m-3/4}$, by a windowed Fourier transform in $u$ (frequency
resolution $\rieszRes$). The zeros of $L(s,\chi_{-4})$ were located independently
($\gamma=\rieszLone,\rieszLtwo,\rieszLthree,\dots$). The result is a clear null: after detrending the
spectrum, the power at the predicted frequencies $\gamma/2$ is statistically indistinguishable from the
power at control and at random frequencies. This is exactly what Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothed} predicts.
The off-diagonal part contributes $O(H)$ to the Ces\`aro sum, and it fluctuates: it is a sum of
fractional parts over the differences of the roots of $f$ modulo $d$, and these fluctuations are of
size $x$ in $R_1$, against $x^{1/4}$ for the zero terms of the diagonal. The zeros are buried under the
off-diagonal.

\emph{Diagonal sum.} Proposition~\ref{thm:explicit} concerns the diagonal alone, which is computable
exactly (via $\omega_f(p)=1+\chi_{-4}(p)$) for $n\le\rdX$. Here the residual after the smooth terms has
the size the theorem predicts ($\approx0.1\,x^{m-3/4}$ in amplitude), and its strongest spectral peaks
lie near predicted frequencies (Table~\ref{tab:rieszd}), but the predicted frequencies are dense
(about one per $0.8$ in $\omega$) while the resolution is $\rdRes$, so proximity proves little. Two
sharper tests are both negative at this range: the detrended power at the predicted frequencies in the
band $4\le\omega\le24$ averages $\rdZL$ (zeros of $L$) and $\rdZZ$ (zeros of $\zeta$) in units of the
spectrum's scatter, against $\rdZR\pm\rdZRsd$ at random frequencies in the same band; and a matched
filter, fitting cosines and sines at $\gamma/2$ for the first $\mfNL$ zeros of $L(s,\chi_{-4})$ and
$\mfNZ$ of $\zeta$, explains $\mfRpred\%$ of the residual variance, while the same set of frequencies
shifted by $0.15$--$0.6$ explains $\mfRctrlMean\%$ on average and up to $\mfRctrlMax\%$.

\subsection{The Galois group}\label{sec:galois}

The off-diagonal Dirichlet series $\sum_dd^{-s}W_f(d)\sum_{s\ne s'}\zeta(s,\langle(s'-s)/d\rangle)$
(Hurwitz zeta functions of the root differences) has, at $s=0$, a polar part of order at most $r_f-2$
times the bias of the distribution of the root differences, where $r_f$ is the number of orbits of the
Galois group on ordered pairs of roots; $r_f=2$ exactly when the group is $2$-transitive. For
$r_f\ge3$ (cyclic cubics, $D_4$, $C_4$ and $V_4$ quartics) a biased distribution of the differences of
distinct roots modulo $d$ could therefore add a logarithm to $\Sigma_f(H)$ and break the universality
of $-\tfrac12$. We tested this on $\galNonN$ polynomials with non-$2$-transitive groups (Shanks' simplest
cubics $t^3-at^2-(a+3)t-1$, $t^4-c$, and $C_4$, $V_4$ quartics; the group was verified from the
distribution of $\omega_f(p)$) against $\galTwoN$ with $S_3$ or $S_4$ (Table~\ref{tab:galois};
\texttt{research/experiments/galois.ts}). The slope of $\Sigma_f(H)/C$ over $10^3\le H\le10^5$ averages
$\galNonMean\pm\galNonSd$ for the non-$2$-transitive groups and $\galTwoMean\pm\galTwoSd$ for the
$2$-transitive ones. The means agree with each other and with the finite-$H$ value expected from
Section~\ref{sec:sumnum}; the scatter between individual polynomials is $3$--$5$ times larger when the
group is not $2$-transitive, which is what a higher-order polar part with fluctuating coefficient
produces. The constant $-\tfrac12$ appears to be independent of the Galois group; the rate of
approach to it is not.

\section{Primes along polynomial sequences}\label{sec:primes}

\emph{Summary: primes along polynomial sequences fluctuate less than a random model of the same density,
and the deficit matches the pair-correlation prediction cell by cell (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).}
Everything in this section is conditional on the Hardy--Littlewood pair conjecture for $f$, in the
sense explained in the introduction: we test whether primes behave as~\eqref{eq:varHL} predicts when
Conjecture~\ref{conj} is inserted. With $\mathbf 1_t$, $p_t$, $w_t$, $A_T$, $V_T$ as in the
introduction and $\mu_T=\sum_{t<T}p_t$, the prediction is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:varHL2}
\Var(A_T)\approx V_T+2\sum_{h=1}^{T-1}\bigl(S_f(h)-C(f)^2\bigr)W_T(h),\qquad W_T(h)=\sum_{t<T-h}w_tw_{t+h},
\end{equation}
which is~\eqref{eq:varHL}; for $f(t)=t$, inserting $\sum_{h\le H}\Sig(h)=H-\tfrac12\log H+O(1)$ with
$w_t\approx1/\log X$ gives~\eqref{eq:MS}.

\paragraph{Design.} For a polynomial $f$ and a length $T$ we count the primes $A$ among
$f(t_0),\dots,f(t_0+T-1)$ and form the dispersion $\Phi=(A-\mu)^2/V$ with $\mu=\mu_T$, $V=V_T$ as
above; averaged over the polynomials of a cell, $\Phi=1$ for independent trials with the
Bateman--Horn probabilities, $\Phi<1$ for a variance deficit. $\Phi_{\mathrm{HL}}$ is the average of
$\Var_{\mathrm{HL}}/V$ from~\eqref{eq:varHL2}. Three families are used, $\nPoly$ polynomials each per
degree: arithmetic progressions ($2\le q\le300$); spiral polynomials of degree $2$ to $6$ with base
point in a box of radius $\le20$; random integer polynomials of degree $2$ to $6$ as above. $T$ runs
over $25,50,\dots$ up to $3200$ ($d\le2$), $1600$ ($d=3$), $800$, $400$, $200$; the values reach
$10^{16}$ and primality is decided by Miller--Rabin with the first twelve prime bases (deterministic
below $3.3\cdot10^{23}$). The Euler products for $C$ and $S_f$ are truncated at $p\le20000$
($d\le2$), $8000$ ($d=3$), $3000$ otherwise. Standard errors are bootstrap over the polynomials of a
cell. As a control, for every polynomial and $T$ the same number of integers is drawn uniformly at random
from $[f(t_0),f(t_0+T)]$ and passed through the identical primality and weighting code; its dispersion
$\Phi_{\mathrm{rand}}$ must be $1$. Everything is generated by \texttt{research/experiments/variance.ts} from a fixed
seed.

deficit is the lower-order term of~\eqref{eq:law}, and it is polynomial-dependent: the two families of

$p\le20000$ they are positive and correct. $\Sigma_f(H)$ inherits the conditional convergence of the
Euler product, and for five test quadratics moving the truncation from $5000$ to $60000$ still changes
$\Sigma_f(3200)$ by $5$--$20\%$ (\texttt{research/experiments/trunc.ts}); numerical work on second moments of prime
values of polynomials must control this (see~\cite{Volfson2026} for tail estimates).

\section{Polynomials of the $d$-dimensional Ulam spiral}\label{sec:spiral}

\emph{This section records the origin of the project; the reader interested only in the analytic results
can skip it.} Our ``spiral'' families come from the following construction, which is of independent interest because it
turns the familiar Ulam picture~\cite{Stein1964} into a generator of polynomial families in every degree.

\begin{definition}\label{def:spiral}
Let $\sigma_2:\N\to\Z^2$ be the Ulam spiral ($\sigma_2(1)=(0,0)$, $\sigma_2(2)=(1,0)$,
$\sigma_2(3)=(1,1)$, counter-clockwise), so that ring $k$ carries the integers $(2k-1)^2+1,\dots,(2k+1)^2$.
For $d\ge3$, shell $k$ of $\Z^d$ (Chebyshev norm $k$) carries $(2k-1)^d+1,\dots,(2k+1)^d$: the
$(d-1)$-dimensional shell of radius $k$ is swept through the new axis $x_d$ in the layer order
$0,+1,-1,\dots,\pm(k-1)$, each layer in the order of $\sigma_{d-1}$, and the caps $x_d=\pm k$ are then
filled with the $(d-1)$-dimensional ball of radius $k$ in the order of $\sigma_{d-1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:poly}
For $b\in\Z^d$ and $v\in\{-1,0,1\}^d\setminus\{0\}$ there are $t_0\le2\max_i|b_i|+2$ and
$f_{b,v}\in\Q[t]$ of degree exactly $d$, with leading coefficient $2^d$ and denominator dividing $d!$,
such that the integer at $b+tv$ equals $f_{b,v}(t)$ for all $t\ge t_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $t>2\max|b_i|+1$ the order of the $|b_i+tv_i|$ is fixed, so the shell index
$k(t)=\max_i|b_i+tv_i|$ is linear in $t$ with slope $1$, and the point stays in one face (band or cap)
of the shell with a fixed layer-sign. By induction on $d$ the offset of the point within its shell is a
polynomial in $k$ of degree at most $d-1$: for $d=2$ it is the position along one ring side; for
$d\ge3$ it is $\ell\,s_{d-1}(k)+\mathrm{off}_{d-1}$ in a band, with $\ell$ the layer index (linear in
$t$) and $s_{d-1}(k)=(2k+1)^{d-1}-(2k-1)^{d-1}$, and $(2k-1)s_{d-1}(k)+\varepsilon(2k+1)^{d-1}+n_{d-1}-1$
in a cap, where $\mathrm{off}_{d-1}$ and $n_{d-1}$ are the corresponding quantities of the projected ray.
Hence the integer is $(2k(t)-1)^d+O(k^{d-1})$, of degree $d$ in $t$ with leading coefficient $2^d$; the
coefficients are rational, and the denominator of an integer-valued polynomial of degree $d$ divides
$d!$.
\end{proof}

Before $t_0$ a ray runs through a lower-degree transient inside one shell and possibly through other
degree-$d$ regimes; only the eventual regime governs the far part of the line. Our detector locates it
by exact finite differences at $t\ge2\max|b_i|+2$ and was checked on $\tOneRays$ random rays in
dimensions $2$--$7$. The spiral was the starting point of this work: the author asked whether its
``pseudo-patterns'' become polynomial in higher dimensions, whether the Fibonacci dimensions are special,
and whether the patterns might be quasiperiodic. Theorem~\ref{thm:poly} answers the first question; the
other two have negative answers, documented in the repository (\texttt{research/experiments/REPORT-full.md}): along
random rays the number of primes agrees with Bateman--Horn within one standard error in every dimension,
the line-to-line dispersion of prime counts inside a box is reproduced by the spread of the constants
$C(f)$ alone, the Fibonacci dimensions are indistinguishable from their neighbours, and the diffraction
pattern of the primes on the spiral consists of rational Bragg peaks over a diffuse background. The
visible pattern of a prime spiral in any dimension is the function $\text{line}\mapsto C(f_{\text{line}})$
and nothing else; the one thing the spiral revealed that is not in the constants is the variance deficit
of Sections~\ref{sec:sum} and~\ref{sec:primes}, a property of polynomial sequences and not of the geometry.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{Summary.} The message of the paper in one sentence: the fluctuation of the number of prime
values of a polynomial is governed, at second order, by an explicit arithmetic function whose generating
series is the Dedekind zeta function of the field cut out by the polynomial, and the resulting variance
deficit is $\tfrac12C(f)\log H$, universal in form and linear in $C(f)$.

\paragraph{What is proved and what is not.} What is proved is Theorems~\ref{thm:expansion}--\ref{thm:smoothed}, \ref{thm:ff} and~\ref{thm:poly}:
the exact identity, the Dedekind-zeta generating function of the diagonal with residue $1/C(f)$, the
symmetric-square $L$-function inside it, the closed formula for quadratics, the Ces\`aro form of
Conjecture~\ref{conj} for linear $f$ and its reduction for general $f$ to $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f=o(H\log H)$
(Hypothesis~(E) for the constant term), and the vanishing of the small-modulus off-diagonal over
$\F_q[u]$ together with the finite support of what remains (Lemma~\ref{lem:support}). Proposition~\ref{thm:explicit} rests on transplanting the proof of Goldston and Suriajaya,
which we have sketched but not written out. What is numerical is the rest: for $\exN$ quadratics computed
exactly to $H=10^6$ with $C$ spanning more than a decade the slope of the Ces\`aro sum $\Sigma^\ast_f$ scales as
$C^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha=\exCesAlpha\pm\exCesAlphaSe$ and coefficient $\exCesK$ against the predicted $\tfrac12$,
excluding the $C^2$ law (the sharp sum gives $\alpha=\exAlpha\pm\exAlphaSe$ with a scatter set by its
oscillation in $H$); the off-diagonal remainder of
quadratics is bounded to $10^5$ and the function-field tail to $N=\ffNmax$; and the prime counts along
polynomial sequences have the variance that~\eqref{eq:varHL2} predicts, with the finite-$T$ excess of the
deficit accounted for by the constant terms $A_f$.

Three consequences and open problems. (i)~The Montgomery--Soundararajan law $\Var/\E\to1-\log T/\log X$
is universal for polynomial prime values, but the approach to it is slow and polynomial-dependent;
any numerical or heuristic study of the second moment at accessible $T$ must include $A_f$. The
smoothed diagonal gives $A_f$ in terms of the constant $B_f$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet}, i.e.\ of
$E_f'(0)/E_f(0)$ and the residue of $\zeta_K$, up to the $O(1)$ contribution of the off-diagonal.
(ii)~Proving the leading term of Conjecture~\ref{conj} for a $2$-transitive Galois group is now the
problem of saving an unbounded factor over the trivial bound for $\mathrm{Off}^\ast_f$
(Remark~\ref{rem:two}); the constant term needs Hypothesis~(E). For quadratics the roots modulo $d$ are
controlled by Kloosterman sums (Hooley; Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec), and this is the case to attempt
first; over $\F_q[u]$ Theorem~\ref{thm:ff} reduces everything to the moduli of degree $>N$, where
Deligne's theorem applies as $q\to\infty$. (iii)~The Dedekind zeta function. For $f(t)=t$ the
error term in the Riesz means of $\sum_{h\le x}\Sig(h)$ is an explicit formula over the zeros of
$\zeta$~\cite{GoldstonSuriajaya2021}, the zeros entering through the factor $1/\zeta(2s)$ that the
squarefree support of the Ramanujan-sum expansion produces. The expansion~\eqref{eq:expansion} has the
same shape, with $D_f(s)=\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$ generating the diagonal and the squarefree support
producing, inside $E_f$, Euler factors of the form $1-c_p\,p^{-2-2s}$ with $c_p$ a quadratic expression
in $\omega_f(p)$; working out the corresponding explicit formula would show that the fluctuations of the pair-correlation sum, hence
of the conjectured variance of the prime values of $f$, are governed by the zeros of $\zeta_K$ (and of
the $L$-function of the permutation representation of the Galois group of $f$ on pairs of roots, which
enters through the $p^{-2}$ terms of the local factors). This is the precise sense in which a Riemann
hypothesis, that of the field cut out by a lattice line's polynomial, governs the primes on that line.
By contrast a direct analogue of the Goldston--Montgomery equivalence~\cite{GoldstonMontgomery1987} is
not available, because prime values of a non-linear polynomial are not the support of a Dirichlet
series. Finally, the framework
of~\cite{MontgomerySoundararajan2004} predicts Gaussian fluctuations only for $H\le X^{1-\delta}$; along
a polynomial sequence $T$ is at most $X^{1/d}$, so the restriction is automatically satisfied, and the
higher moments should be computable by the same means.
