\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Between \FirstDay{} and \LastDay{} a software engineer with no research background in analytic number theory
produced, with a large language model doing almost all of the mathematical, computational and textual work,
a series of four research papers on the pair singular series of prime values of polynomials
(\PagesI{}, \PagesII{}, \PagesIII{} and \PagesIV{} pages). The mathematics is not the subject of this paper. Its subject is
the \emph{process}: how a test-driven engineering habit and a small set of structural guardrails (a public
knowledge base of state and errors, a project-wide errata file, adversarial readings by fresh model instances,
external assessments archived against the revision they read, a two-routes rule for every constant, and a
stopping criterion) turned an inherently unreliable generator of mathematics into a process whose output can be
checked, and was checked, by people who did not take part in it. We document the set-up, the evolution of the
prompting, the guardrails and when each was introduced, the complete record of \NFallacies{} recorded
fallacies and \NErrata{} errata items, who caught which class of error, and what the process could not catch. The
programme's mathematical status is reported exactly as its own errata report it: theorems proved and read,
theorems retracted, and problems left open.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The question.} Large language models can write mathematics that looks right. The question this case
study addresses is narrower and more useful than whether they can do research: under what \emph{process} does a
non-specialist, working with such a model, produce mathematical output whose correctness does not depend on
trusting either of them? The programme documented here is one data point, with a complete public record, and
this paper is the reading of that record.

\paragraph{What is claimed and what is not.} We claim that the process described in \S\ref{sec:guardrails} produced
(i) a body of work in which every statement is labelled proved, conditional, conjectural or open, (ii) a record of
every error found, by whom, and how it was fixed, and (iii) numbers that can be regenerated from the repository. We
do \emph{not} claim that the mathematics is important, nor that it is correct beyond what its errata say; as of
this writing the four papers have been read adversarially by model instances without session context and by two
outside readers, and not yet by a specialist in the field (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The case study has $n=1$.

\paragraph{The subject programme in one paragraph.} For an irreducible polynomial $f$, the pair singular series
$S_f(h)$ is the explicit weight that, under the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture, governs simultaneous primality of
$f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$. Paper~I proves an exact identity for $\sum_{h\le H}(S_f(h)-C(f)^2)$, splits it into a diagonal
whose Dirichlet series is $\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$ and an off-diagonal, and states a conjecture in Ces\`aro form.
Paper~II identifies $E_f$ as an infinite product of Artin $L$-functions and proves a conditional explicit formula.
Paper~III decomposes the off-diagonal into pieces and proves everything except one window. Paper~IV finds, and
partly proves, that the pieces oscillate at the spectral parameters of even Maass forms. Nothing in the four papers
proves anything about primes without Hardy--Littlewood, and nothing bears on the Riemann Hypothesis.

\section{Set-up}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The author}
The author is a freelance software engineer, entrepreneur and trader running several projects at once, with a
bachelor's degree in software engineering. The hardest mathematics course of that degree was stochastics; Fourier
transforms and Markov chains were fluent at the time, and professional work had included specialised algorithms
(for instance enumerating all cycles in a weighted bidirectional graph up to a given size). No analytic number
theory. What the author could read in a number-theory paper on 8~September was the prose, the meaning of an
asymptotic statement and why it matters, and concepts such as the Bateman--Horn constant $C(f)$ and the comparison of
polynomials by their arithmetic; not the proofs. The curiosity about primes was old, and the prime-rich diagonals of
the Ulam spiral were the visual intuition from which the study started. \evidence{Interview Q1}


\subsection{The tools}
The work was done in a Linux terminal with the Claude Code agent driving Claude Fable~5.1 throughout, with Claude
Opus~5 for simpler tasks, as the disclosure sections of the four papers state. Fresh model instances without access to the working session were used as adversarial readers
(\S\ref{sec:guardrails}); the cheaper model was used for such readings when rate limits made this necessary. The
author reviewed the output with models of other vendors, mostly OpenAI's GPT and Google's Gemini, instructing them
to take a hostile position; one such assessment (ChatGPT, 12~September) is archived as a PDF against the revision
it read, and a second outside screening (a separate Claude instance, 14~September) found the defect recorded as
ERRATA~26. For orientation the author used ChatGPT, Gemini, web search, Mathematics Stack Exchange and the arXiv;
a good part of the background knowledge came from popular mathematics videos (Numberphile).
\evidence{Interview Q2; \file{research/reviews/}; ERRATA 11--15, 26}

The author worked about eight hours a day for the six days. The total computation cost was about
\$150. \evidence{Interview Q2}

Software: TypeScript (\file{tsx}) for sieves and exact rational arithmetic, Python with \file{mpmath} for high
precision, \file{vitest} for the workbench tests, \LaTeX{} via \file{tectonic}, and downloaded Maass-form
coefficients from the LMFDB. Every number in the four papers is produced by a script in the repository.

The author's prior experience with language models predates this project by years: an early adopter who had
worked with GPT-3 in its closed beta, and who describes ``a vast experience in working with AI output''. This is
the one qualification the case study cannot control for. \evidence{Interview Q2}

\section{From a visualisation tool to a research programme}\label{sec:origin}

The programme did not start as one. It started as a web application for looking at prime spirals in higher
dimensions, built to test a private thesis (that Ulam-spiral pseudo-patterns become polynomial patterns in higher
dimensions, that Fibonacci dimensions are special, and that this might say something about the Riemann
Hypothesis). Two engineering decisions on the first day shaped everything after:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{The thesis was written as a test suite.} Each claim became a hypothesis with a pass/fail criterion,
  a deterministic seed, a raw report (\file{REPORT.md}) and a separate reading (\file{CONCLUSIONS.md}). Three of the
  four claims failed the tests within hours; the tool had been built to falsify, and it did.
  \evidence{\file{research/experiments/CONCLUSIONS.md}}
\item \textbf{The honest framing was agreed inside the tool.} The theory panel of the application stated that
  spiral patterns are a Bateman--Horn main-term phenomenon, that the Riemann Hypothesis is an error-term
  statement, and that no link between them is known. This sentence survived into every paper.
\end{enumerate}
The one lead the tests left standing, a sub-Poisson variance along polynomial rays, is the Montgomery--Soundararajan
second moment for polynomial prime values; its diagonal weight $\omega_f(p)/(p-\omega_f(p))$ is where paper~I began.
\paragraph{The turn.} The author asked a short literature question (who had worked on this) and found little.
From a software engineer's viewpoint the problem ``was not super hard to solve with AI'': working test-driven, one
could try to dig deeper where Bateman--Horn stopped. As soon as something of interest appeared, the instruction
became to build a paper, and possibly a research programme if the findings turned out to be interesting. The model's
stated expectation at that point was to expect nothing, but that experiments can be valuable in this field.
\evidence{Interview Q3}

\section{How the prompting developed}\label{sec:prompting}

The repository contains no prompts; this section is written from the interview and from the author's
turns preserved in the session transcripts. \todo{Quote three or four actual prompts once the author has selected
them from the transcripts.}

\subsection{Stage 1: the framing (8 September)}
The first prompt defined the enterprise as a research programme, to be run test-driven, with methods and paths
enumerated and rated, and with a knowledge file collecting the findings (\S\ref{sec:guardrails}). The thesis itself
was handed over as four claims and came back as a test suite that refuted three of them the same evening. The
author did not defend the claims; the surviving lead was pursued instead. \evidence{Interview Q3--Q5; \file{CONCLUSIONS.md}}

\subsection{Stage 2: the persona and the register (9--10 September)}
The author describes the working image: ``an absolute pro mathematician who never bulges to my requests but
patiently works with me through the problems and explains them in a way that I, as a software engineer, can build
a research framework around, or tell it when I think we should hold, do more tests, a review.'' When the author
understood nothing of a passage, the instruction was to simplify until a decision could be made: which deeper path
to follow, whether more literature was needed. Vagueness in the agent's answers was treated as a signal to ask for
literature. The vocabulary that appears in the commit log of these days (``reviewer round'', ``rigorous pass'',
``literature round, verified'') is the trace of this: each is a request the author could formulate without
mathematics. \evidence{Interview Q6; \texttt{git log} 9--10 Sep}

\subsection{Stage 3: outside hostility, compressed and returned (9--13 September)}
Decisions the author could not check mathematically were routed through review: the papers were given to other
vendors' models with the instruction to take a hostile position and find errors; the findings were compressed,
annotated with context, and returned to the working agent; the agent's response was discussed with the reviewer
again. The author notes that this is how peer review works. The external assessment of 12~September, whose three
findings all stood, is the largest instance; ERRATA~26 the latest. For the choice of repair route in paper~IV the
author relied on this loop together with the data (the divisor-set test that showed the level-2 lines predicted by
the repair), not on an independent reading of the proofs. \evidence{Interview Q7; KNOWLEDGE \S0c}

\subsection{Stage 4: effort, and knowing when to stop (12--14 September)}
Two opposite corrections of the model's judgement of effort occurred. The author repeatedly pressed the model when
it estimated ``at least two weeks of work'', asserting that for a model it is a few hours and demanding the attempt;
by the author's account this was decisive in paper~IV, where the level-$\Gamma_0(e)$ repair and the smooth-window
theorem were produced in one day. In the other direction, the model's estimate that the remainder bound of paper~IV
was ``a day of work'' was wrong: the bound is open, and the error is recorded as fallacy F33. \evidence{Interview
Q8; ERRATA 16--17; F33}

The author's one regret concerns rigour rather than error: by the fourth paper the process demanded more
verification than the situation warranted, sessions ran out of context, and work had to be restarted; ``one should
keep the context somewhat human, not absolute superhuman.'' The question ``are we over-rigoring?'' produced the
written stopping criterion. \evidence{Interview Q8--Q9; KNOWLEDGE \S0b}
\interview{9}{Still open: the restart after running out of context: one episode or several, which day and paper?}

\paragraph{Who caught what.} Internal readings (the model re-reading its own work in a fresh instance) caught
algebraic slips, convergence errors and inconsistencies between sections, reliably and in quantity. The two outside
readers caught what internal readings did not: a claim that is true in the special case and false in general (the
assessment's finding on paper~II), a sum rewritten as an orbit sum over a set that is not an orbit (paper~IV), and a
method invoked with a hypothesis it does not have (paper~I, ERRATA~26). The second outside reader's own assessment of
this asymmetry is quoted in \S\ref{sec:limits}. \evidence{ERRATA 11--15, 18, 22, 24--26}

\paragraph{Two retractions.} The programme retracted two statements it had believed: the sharp form of
Conjecture~1 (there is no bounded remainder at all, F26) and the level-one form of paper~IV's theorem (the object
is a signed sum over $\Gamma_0(e)$-orbits, not one orbit, F28). Both are recorded with the reasoning that had
produced them.
The author reports no ``worst moment'' attached to any of these errors; the retractions were absorbed by the
process that had been set up to absorb them. \evidence{Interview Q9}

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verification}

What ``verified'' means in this programme, from the checklist in the knowledge base (\S6):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item Every exact constant by two independent routes.
\item Every explicit formula by exact subtraction, at most one fitted coefficient, and comparison with shifted
  controls (a fit that also matches a wrong frequency proves nothing).
\item Every theorem by at least one adversarial reading by an instance that did not write it.
\item Every prediction with no free parameter tested against data before it is called confirmed: paper~IV's
  smooth-window theorem predicted the amplitude and phase of three spectral lines on two objects from LMFDB
  coefficients alone, and the data matched to a few percent and a few hundredths of a radian (ERRATA 21--23).
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{What the author verified.} The author read the code of some of the tests, ran them often, and asked the
model to ``reflect'' on the results and look for logical fallacies and flawed outcomes; the mathematics of the
proofs was not checked by the author, and the author says so. The author never refused a claim the model insisted
on; the standing instruction was ``excellence and simplicity, a kind of humbleness'', which, in the author's view,
worked against the pace at times: the model asked for human input more often than was needed. \evidence{Interview Q10}

\section{What the process cannot do}\label{sec:limits}

The second outside reader, after finding the paper~I defect, wrote an assessment of the limits of such readings
that we adopt as the honest statement of the limits of this whole process. Paraphrased: the attacks a model
reader can mount are algebra it can re-derive, numerics it can re-run, and consistency it can grep for; the
failure mode that matters most, a borrowed theorem invoked outside its hypotheses, is detectable only by someone
who has read the sources; its own finding was possible only because Dedekind's conjecture and Siegel zeros are
famous; Shiu's exact uniformity conditions and Katok--Sarnak's normalisation are not, and neither the reader nor the
author can check them. \todo{Quote verbatim with the author's permission.}

Consequently, the state of the mathematics is: every statement has survived at least two adversarial passes and,
for the core of papers~I--II, one external assessment; the class of error that remains possible is known and
named; the only remaining move is review by a specialist who knows the sources. This paper does not claim more.

\section{Recommendations}\label{sec:recommend}

The list below is the author's recipe as stated in the interview, ordered by the author's own weighting, with
the record's additions marked.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item Declare the enterprise in the first prompt: a research programme, test-driven, with the methods and paths to
  be enumerated and rated, and a knowledge file that collects findings, chronology and known fallacies. The author
  considers this the decisive step; it forces the model's context into compacted notes of its own work.
\item A computational check for everything, and a written record of the way of thought.
\item Ask for excellence, simplicity and humility, and for explanations at the level at which you can decide.
\item Route what you cannot check through hostile outside review: other models, instructed to attack; compress the
  findings, annotate them, return them to the working agent, and discuss again.
\item Press on effort estimates in both directions: demand the attempt when the model says ``weeks''; record it when
  the model says ``a day'' and is wrong.
\item (From the record.) Decide the repair principle before the repair: correct, or leave open; never a weaker claim
  about a different object.
\item (From the record.) Set the stopping criterion in writing before you need it; keep the demanded rigour at a
  human scale.
\item (From the record.) Know the class of error the process cannot catch, say so in the papers, and route the work
  to someone who can.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The author's claim.} The author states the purpose of this case study as a role model ``for how to work
scientifically in very complex domains while maintaining scientific rigour'', for anyone working in science with AI,
wanting to, or refusing to. The work met hostility when first presented, and nobody checked it at first, on the
assumption that it was machine-generated noise; the author understands this reaction and agrees with the recent
statement on AI and mathematics signed by many leading mathematicians \todo{cite correctly once the author supplies
title and URL}. The programme is offered as a demonstration that a workable toolkit exists for embedding AI so that
the quality of its output rises and the output becomes easier to falsify: test-driven development borrowed from
software engineering, and the enumeration and rating of the available methods. \evidence{Interview Q11--Q12}

\paragraph{On authorship.} The author at first wanted the model as co-author, and abandoned this because no journal
accepts it and because the model is not a persona; not knowing how to credit it, the author chose complete
transparency about the flow of work instead, of which this paper is part. \evidence{Interview Q11; ERRATA 8}
