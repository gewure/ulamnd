\title{A verifiable process for AI-assisted mathematical research:\\ a case study}
\author{Jasper Reichardt}
\date{Version 1, 14 September 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Between \FirstDay{} and \LastDay{} a software engineer with no background in analytic number theory worked with a
large language model, which did most of the mathematical, computational and textual work, on a series of four
draft papers about the pair singular series of prime values of polynomials. Whether that mathematics is correct or
worth anything is not settled and is not the subject of this paper; the drafts have been read adversarially by
model instances and by two outside readers, not yet by a specialist. The subject is the process: a test-driven
engineering habit and a small set of written guardrails (a knowledge file of state and known fallacies, a
project-wide errata file that is never shortened, readings by fresh model instances, outside assessments archived
with the revision they read, two independent routes for every constant, a stopping criterion) that were meant to
make the output checkable by people who took no part in it, and to make its errors visible rather than hidden. We
describe the set-up, how the prompting developed, when each guardrail was introduced and why, the record of
\NFallacies{} fallacies and \NErrata{} errata items with who caught which class of error, and the class of error the process
did not catch. The mathematical status is reported as the programme's own errata report it: some theorems proved
and read, two statements retracted, the central problems open. The study has one case and one author, and its
conclusions are offered as an experience report, not as a method with demonstrated generality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The question.} Large language models can write mathematics that looks right. The question this case
study addresses is narrower and more useful than whether they can do research: under what \emph{process} does a
non-specialist, working with such a model, produce mathematical output whose correctness does not depend on
trusting either of them? The programme documented here is one data point, with a complete public record, and
this paper is the reading of that record.

\paragraph{What is claimed and what is not.} We claim that the process described in \S\ref{sec:guardrails} produced
(i) a body of work in which every statement is labelled proved, conditional, conjectural or open, (ii) a record of
every error found, by whom, and how it was fixed, and (iii) numbers that can be regenerated from the repository. We
do \emph{not} claim that the mathematics is important, nor that it is correct beyond what its errata say; as of
this writing the four papers have been read adversarially by model instances without session context and by two
outside readers, and not yet by a specialist in the field (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The case study has $n=1$.

\paragraph{The subject programme in one paragraph.} For an irreducible polynomial $f$, the pair singular series
$S_f(h)$ is the explicit weight that, under the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture, governs simultaneous primality of
$f(t)$ and $f(t+h)$. Paper~I proves an exact identity for $\sum_{h\le H}(S_f(h)-C(f)^2)$, splits it into a diagonal
whose Dirichlet series is $\zeta_K(s+1)E_f(s)$ and an off-diagonal, and states a conjecture in Ces\`aro form.
Paper~II identifies $E_f$ as an infinite product of Artin $L$-functions and proves a conditional explicit formula.
Paper~III decomposes the off-diagonal into pieces and proves everything except one window. Paper~IV finds, and
partly proves, that the pieces oscillate at the spectral parameters of even Maass forms. Nothing in the four papers
proves anything about primes without Hardy--Littlewood, and nothing bears on the Riemann Hypothesis.

\section{Set-up}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The author}
The author is a freelance software engineer, entrepreneur and trader running several projects at once, with a
bachelor's degree in software engineering. The hardest mathematics course of that degree was stochastics; Fourier
transforms and Markov chains were fluent at the time, and professional work had included specialised algorithms
(for instance enumerating all cycles in a weighted bidirectional graph up to a given size). No analytic number
theory. What the author could read in a number-theory paper on 8~September was the prose, the meaning of an
asymptotic statement and why it matters, and concepts such as the Bateman--Horn constant $C(f)$ and the comparison of
polynomials by their arithmetic; not the proofs. The curiosity about primes was old, and the prime-rich diagonals of
the Ulam spiral were the visual intuition from which the study started. \evidence{Interview Q1}


\subsection{The tools}
The work was done in a Linux terminal with the Claude Code agent driving Claude Fable~5.1 throughout, with Claude
Opus~5 for simpler tasks, as the disclosure sections of the four papers state. Fresh model instances without access to the working session were used as adversarial readers
(\S\ref{sec:guardrails}); the cheaper model was used for such readings when rate limits made this necessary. The
author reviewed the output with models of other vendors, mostly OpenAI's GPT and Google's Gemini, instructing them
to take a hostile position; one such assessment (ChatGPT, 12~September) is archived as a PDF against the revision
it read, and a second outside screening (a separate Claude instance, 14~September) found the defect recorded as
ERRATA~26. For orientation the author used ChatGPT, Gemini, web search, Mathematics Stack Exchange and the arXiv;
a good part of the background knowledge came from popular mathematics videos (Numberphile).
\evidence{Interview Q2; \file{research/reviews/}; ERRATA 11--15, 26}

The author worked about eight hours a day for the six days. The total computation cost was about
\$150. \evidence{Interview Q2}

Software: TypeScript (\file{tsx}) for sieves and exact rational arithmetic, Python with \file{mpmath} for high
precision, \file{vitest} for the workbench tests, \LaTeX{} via \file{tectonic}, and downloaded Maass-form
coefficients from the LMFDB. Every number in the four papers is produced by a script in the repository.

The author's prior experience with language models predates this project by years: an early adopter who had
worked with GPT-3 in its closed beta, and who describes ``a vast experience in working with AI output''. This is
the one qualification the case study cannot control for. \evidence{Interview Q2}

\section{From a visualisation tool to a research programme}\label{sec:origin}

The programme did not start as one. It started as a web application for looking at prime spirals in higher
dimensions, built to test a private thesis (that Ulam-spiral pseudo-patterns become polynomial patterns in higher
dimensions, that Fibonacci dimensions are special, and that this might say something about the Riemann
Hypothesis). Two engineering decisions on the first day shaped everything after:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{The thesis was written as a test suite.} Each claim became a hypothesis with a pass/fail criterion,
  a deterministic seed, a raw report (\file{REPORT.md}) and a separate reading (\file{CONCLUSIONS.md}). Three of the
  four claims failed the tests within hours; the tool had been built to falsify, and it did.
  \evidence{\file{research/experiments/CONCLUSIONS.md}}
\item \textbf{The honest framing was agreed inside the tool.} The theory panel of the application stated that
  spiral patterns are a Bateman--Horn main-term phenomenon, that the Riemann Hypothesis is an error-term
  statement, and that no link between them is known. This sentence survived into every paper.
\end{enumerate}
The one lead the tests left standing, a sub-Poisson variance along polynomial rays, is the Montgomery--Soundararajan
second moment for polynomial prime values; its diagonal weight $\omega_f(p)/(p-\omega_f(p))$ is where paper~I began.
\paragraph{The turn.} The author asked a short literature question (who had worked on this) and found little.
From a software engineer's viewpoint the problem ``was not super hard to solve with AI'': working test-driven, one
could try to dig deeper where Bateman--Horn stopped. As soon as something of interest appeared, the instruction
became to build a paper, and possibly a research programme if the findings turned out to be interesting. The model's
stated expectation at that point was to expect nothing, but that experiments can be valuable in this field.
\evidence{Interview Q3}

\paragraph{The author's own account of why this works.} In the author's view the knowledge base and the fallacy
list ``force the model's direct context to compacted notes about the work it did'', and this is what raises the
quality: ``these contextual guardrails, notes, chronologies and known fallacies help to recover important high-class
context, so a new session does not have to pick up everything. It is similar to how human notes work.'' The author
sees the success of elaborate agent frameworks as largely the same mechanism. The second half of the recipe is the
engineering habit: ``a computational check for everything, rigorously, and a proper documentation of the way of
thought.'' \evidence{Interview Q4}
\paragraph{Nothing too late; one thing late.} The author does not think any guardrail came too late. What came late
was the restructuring of the repository around the research programme rather than around the web service that
visualises the spiral experiments (the split happened on 9~September, 23:58); the author does not think it had much
influence. The author adds a caution that the record cannot test: every file and every piece of additional context
in the project can influence the quality of the work, and it is possible, though unprovable, that the presence of
the web application's code made the model more efficient. From long experience the author offers two rules of
thumb: ``asking stupid questions gets stupid answers'', and ``a few verbose words at the right time can make a big
difference''. If enough valid anchor points are given, a vague prompt with a lot of imagination can turn into proper
output; the anchor points must be valid. The author's own analogy is that the Heegner points of paper~IV play the
same role inside the mathematics. \evidence{Interview, second round; \texttt{git log} 9 Sep 23:58}

\section{How the prompting developed}\label{sec:prompting}

The repository contains no prompts; this section is written from the interview and from the author's turns
preserved in the session transcripts. At the author's request only fragments of prompts are quoted, all from
13--14~September (spelling as typed):
\begin{quote}\small
``be a bit more exact. we have a series of 4 and want to not deviate from correct paths, merely to correct
something --- we rather leave it open. or \dots{} if we have reason to think we can fix it, to fix it.''\\[2pt]
``consider the series in totality \dots{} i dont want to shy away from the work of doing a bounded but non-trivial
piece of work.''\\[2pt]
``take care for tokens, we are on fable 5, maybe use worse model for simpler task''\\[2pt]
``Elite readability with enough rigor and perfect excellency and also elegancy is kind of the goal.''\\[2pt]
``you always have a Next up. are we not over-rigoring or something? \dots{} At what point should we consider being
finished?''\\[2pt]
``i asked claude to do a hostile screening and it mentions something in paper 1, please investigate and fix, if
true.''
\end{quote}
Each fragment is a decision, a constraint or a routing instruction; none contains mathematics. That is the shape of
the author's part throughout. \evidence{session transcripts, 13--14 Sep}

\subsection{Stage 1: the framing (8 September)}
The first prompt defined the enterprise as a research programme, to be run test-driven, with methods and paths
enumerated and rated, and with a knowledge file collecting the findings (\S\ref{sec:guardrails}). The thesis itself
was handed over as four claims and came back as a test suite that refuted three of them the same evening. The
author did not defend the claims; the surviving lead was pursued instead. \evidence{Interview Q3--Q5; \file{CONCLUSIONS.md}}

\subsection{Stage 2: the persona and the register (9--10 September)}
The author describes the working image: ``an absolute pro mathematician who never bulges to my requests but
patiently works with me through the problems and explains them in a way that I, as a software engineer, can build
a research framework around, or tell it when I think we should hold, do more tests, a review.'' When the author
understood nothing of a passage, the instruction was to simplify until a decision could be made: which deeper path
to follow, whether more literature was needed. Vagueness in the agent's answers was treated as a signal to ask for
literature. The vocabulary that appears in the commit log of these days (``reviewer round'', ``rigorous pass'',
``literature round, verified'') is the trace of this: each is a request the author could formulate without
mathematics. \evidence{Interview Q6; \texttt{git log} 9--10 Sep}

\subsection{Stage 3: outside hostility, compressed and returned (9--13 September)}
Decisions the author could not check mathematically were routed through review: the papers were given to other
vendors' models with the instruction to take a hostile position and find errors; the findings were compressed,
annotated with context, and returned to the working agent; the agent's response was discussed with the reviewer
again. The author notes that this is how peer review works. The external assessment of 12~September, whose three
findings all stood, is the largest instance; ERRATA~26 the latest. For the choice of repair route in paper~IV the
author relied on this loop together with the data (the divisor-set test that showed the level-2 lines predicted by
the repair), not on an independent reading of the proofs. \evidence{Interview Q7; KNOWLEDGE \S0c}

\subsection{Stage 4: effort, and knowing when to stop (12--14 September)}
Two opposite corrections of the model's judgement of effort occurred. The author repeatedly pressed the model when
it estimated ``at least two weeks of work'', asserting that for a model it is a few hours and demanding the attempt;
by the author's account this was decisive in paper~IV, where the level-$\Gamma_0(e)$ repair and the smooth-window
theorem were produced in one day. In the other direction, the model's estimate that the remainder bound of paper~IV
was ``a day of work'' was wrong: the bound is open, and the error is recorded as fallacy F33. \evidence{Interview
Q8; ERRATA 16--17; F33}

The author's one regret concerns rigour rather than error: by the fourth paper the process demanded more
verification than the situation warranted, sessions ran out of context, and work had to be restarted; ``one should
keep the context somewhat human, not absolute superhuman.'' The question ``are we over-rigoring?'' produced the
written stopping criterion. \evidence{Interview Q8--Q9; KNOWLEDGE \S0b}
On restarts the author's view is that they should not happen in a dense situation, and that they can be caused by
running out of budget or by a sub-agent stuck in a loop consuming it; the author regards this as an open problem of
agentic work in general, not a large one in this project. \evidence{Interview, second round}

\paragraph{Who caught what.} Internal readings (the model re-reading its own work in a fresh instance) caught
algebraic slips, convergence errors and inconsistencies between sections, reliably and in quantity. The two outside
readers caught what internal readings did not: a claim that is true in the special case and false in general (the
assessment's finding on paper~II), a sum rewritten as an orbit sum over a set that is not an orbit (paper~IV), and a
method invoked with a hypothesis it does not have (paper~I, ERRATA~26). The second outside reader's own assessment of
this asymmetry is quoted in \S\ref{sec:limits}. \evidence{ERRATA 11--15, 18, 22, 24--26}

\paragraph{Two retractions.} The programme retracted two statements it had believed: the sharp form of
Conjecture~1 (there is no bounded remainder at all, F26) and the level-one form of paper~IV's theorem (the object
is a signed sum over $\Gamma_0(e)$-orbits, not one orbit, F28). Both are recorded with the reasoning that had
produced them.
The author reports no ``worst moment'' attached to any of these errors; the retractions were absorbed by the
process that had been set up to absorb them. \evidence{Interview Q9}

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verification}

What ``verified'' means in this programme, from the checklist in the knowledge base (\S6):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item Every exact constant by two independent routes.
\item Every explicit formula by exact subtraction, at most one fitted coefficient, and comparison with shifted
  controls (a fit that also matches a wrong frequency proves nothing).
\item Every theorem by at least one adversarial reading by an instance that did not write it.
\item Every prediction with no free parameter tested against data before it is called confirmed: paper~IV's
  smooth-window theorem predicted the amplitude and phase of three spectral lines on two objects from LMFDB
  coefficients alone, and the data matched to a few percent and a few hundredths of a radian (ERRATA 21--23).
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{What the author verified.} The author read the code of some of the tests, ran them often, and asked the
model to ``reflect'' on the results and look for logical fallacies and flawed outcomes; the mathematics of the
proofs was not checked by the author, and the author says so. The author never refused a claim the model insisted
on; the standing instruction was ``excellence and simplicity, a kind of humbleness'', which, in the author's view,
worked against the pace at times: the model asked for human input more often than was needed. \evidence{Interview Q10}

\section{What the process cannot do}\label{sec:limits}

The second outside reader, after finding the paper~I defect, wrote an assessment of the limits of such readings
that we adopt as the honest statement of the limits of this whole process. Verbatim, with the author's
permission (the reader is a Claude instance given the papers and nothing else, 14~September):
\begin{quote}\small
``I'm one reader, working from outside the field, with the same blind spots I've had all along --- I proposed the
O(1) fix that turned out to be false, and my numerical check couldn't detect log log growth. The last external
reviewer went three-for-three in three places it looked, and I'd already reviewed those same papers without finding
any of them. My not breaking something is weak evidence, not a verdict.

And the attacks I can mount are structurally limited to the same class: algebra I can re-derive, numerics I can
re-run, internal consistency I can grep for. The failure mode that actually matters here --- a borrowed theorem
invoked outside its hypotheses --- is only detectable by someone who has read the sources. Line 395 is the one
instance I caught, and I caught it because Dedekind and Siegel are famous enough to be in my general knowledge.
Shiu's exact uniformity conditions aren't, and I can't check those. Neither can you.

So the honest scoreboard: the core has now survived two adversarial passes, which is genuinely more than it had a
week ago. And the specific thing that would settle it --- someone who knows whether Shiu, Selberg--Delange and
Katok--Sarnak are being used correctly --- still hasn't happened, and is still the only move on the board.''
\end{quote}
The reader's diagnosis of line~395 was itself only half right (Dedekind's conjecture was not needed; the unproved
non-vanishing claim was, and was also unnecessary; ERRATA~26), which illustrates the point it makes.

Consequently, the state of the mathematics is: every statement has survived at least two adversarial passes and,
for the core of papers~I--II, one external assessment; the class of error that remains possible is known and
named; the only remaining move is review by a specialist who knows the sources. This paper does not claim more.

\section{Recommendations}\label{sec:recommend}

The list below is the author's recipe as stated in the interview, ordered by the author's own weighting, with
the record's additions marked.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item Declare the enterprise in the first prompt: a research programme, test-driven, with the methods and paths to
  be enumerated and rated, and a knowledge file that collects findings, chronology and known fallacies. The author
  regards this as the most important step; in the author's experience it forces the model's context into compacted
  notes of its own work.
\item A computational check for everything, and a written record of the way of thought.
\item Ask for excellence, simplicity and humility, and for explanations at the level at which you can decide.
\item Route what you cannot check through hostile outside review: other models, instructed to attack; compress the
  findings, annotate them, return them to the working agent, and discuss again.
\item Press on effort estimates in both directions: demand the attempt when the model says ``weeks''; record it when
  the model says ``a day'' and is wrong.
\item (From the record.) Decide the repair principle before the repair: correct, or leave open; never a weaker claim
  about a different object.
\item (From the record.) Set the stopping criterion in writing before you need it; keep the demanded rigour at a
  human scale.
\item (From the record.) Know the class of error the process cannot catch, say so in the papers, and route the work
  to someone who can.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The author's claim.} The author states the purpose of this case study as a role model ``for how to work
scientifically in very complex domains while maintaining scientific rigour'', for anyone working in science with AI,
wanting to, or refusing to. The work met hostility when first presented, and nobody checked it at first, on the
assumption that it was machine-generated noise; the author understands this reaction and agrees with the
declaration \emph{A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics}, endorsed by twenty-five Fields medallists.\footnote{
\url{https://mathandai.org/}, accessed 14 September 2026; initial signatories from Avila and Bhargava to Werner and
Zelmanov, including Deligne, Scholze, Tao and Viazovska. The declaration's concern that ``solving problems is only a
tool and proxy for achieving the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight'', and that solutions are
``announced in a rush, leaving no time for a proper writeup, the isolation of new methods and ideas, and citing
relevant previous work of others'', names exactly the failure modes the guardrails of \S\ref{sec:guardrails} were built
against: no number without a script, no claim without a label, no citation without verification, every error kept.}
The author also states what the programme is a case study \emph{of}: the limits of what such a process can currently
do. Beyond this scale it becomes infeasible, and already here even experts will need a long time to understand all
of the output; the author is now learning how to defend and present the work in purely classical mathematical terms.
The programme is offered as a demonstration that a workable toolkit exists for embedding AI so that
the quality of its output rises and the output becomes easier to falsify: test-driven development borrowed from
software engineering, and the enumeration and rating of the available methods. \evidence{Interview Q11--Q12}

\paragraph{On authorship.} The author at first wanted the model as co-author, and abandoned this because no journal
accepts it and because the model is not a persona; not knowing how to credit it, the author chose complete
transparency about the flow of work instead, of which this paper is part. The intended venue for this case study
is the arXiv. \evidence{Interview Q11--Q12; ERRATA 8}

\section{The interview}\label{app:interview}
The questions were put in writing on 14 September 2026 and answered the same day in two rounds. The answers are
reproduced here with spelling and punctuation corrected and a few words supplied in brackets; the unedited text is in
\file{INTERVIEW.md}.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=4pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Q1, background.] I am a freelance software engineer, entrepreneur and trader, running multiple projects. I have
a bachelor's degree in software engineering, and the hardest mathematical course I had to pass was stochastics. What I
can read in number-theory papers is the human-language explanations and the importance of asymptotic proofs. I can
understand concepts such as the Bateman--Horn $C(f)$ quite clearly and know how to compare polynomials conceptually;
during my studies I could do Fourier transforms and Markov chains fluently, and at work I needed to implement
specialised algorithms, for instance to find all cycles in a bidirectional weighted graph up to $N$ vertices. I have
had a curiosity for number theory for a long time, and the prime-richer diagonals of the Ulam spiral were my visual
intuition for where to start a study.
\item[Q2, tools and cost.] I worked about eight hours per day. The tools are the Linux terminal and Claude with
Fable~5.1, and sometimes, for simpler things, Opus. I spent in total about \$150 on computation. I have been an early
adopter of LLMs and worked with GPT-3 in its closed beta already, so I have a vast experience of working with AI
output. I reviewed the output with different AIs, mostly OpenAI's GPT and Gemini. I used ChatGPT, Gemini, Google,
Mathematics Stack Exchange and the arXiv for research; a lot of my knowledge also comes from watching Numberphile
videos.
\item[Q3, the turn.] I asked a short literature question, who had done research on this, and found not a lot; but from
my viewpoint the problem was not super hard to attack with AI. Working test-driven, I thought: let us try to dig
deeper where Bateman--Horn stopped. As soon as I found something of interest I started to instruct [the model] to
build a paper, and possibly a research programme if the findings turned out more interesting. The model told me not
to expect anything, but that experiments can be valuable in this field.
\item[Q4, guardrails.] I would say the first prompt, where I defined that we do a ``research programme'' and work
test-driven, enumerating methods and paths and keeping a \file{KNOWLEDGE.md} where we collect our findings, was the
important part. This usually forces the LLM's direct context into compacted notes about the work it did, and in my
view increases the quality a lot. This is also one reason, in my view, why complex ``agent'' systems are so
successful with what is basically prompt masquerading. These contextual guardrails, notes, chronologies and known
fallacies help to recover important high-class context, so that a new session does not have to pick up everything.
It is similar to how human notes work, and I think this is key to having quality AI output. This, and test-driven:
have a computational check for everything, rigorously, and a proper documentation of the way of thought.

I would not say that any guardrail came too late. I was a bit late to restructure everything around the research
programme rather than the web service that visualises the Ulam experiments, but I do not think it had much influence.
It is important, though, to keep in mind that every file and every piece of additional context in the project can
influence the quality of the work. Maybe, I cannot prove this, the Next.js context helped the model to be more
efficient. This is common prompt philosophy and very hard, if at all possible, to prove. What I can say from my
years of experience is that asking stupid questions gets stupid answers, and that a few verbose words at the right
time can make a big difference. If enough anchor points are given, a vague prompt with a lot of fantasy can turn into
a proper output; the anchor points must be valid. To give an analogy: I think the Heegner points play a similar role
in the work. But I do not understand the work, of course; I am giving my best now to learn how to defend and present
it, purely classically, mathematically.
\item[Q6, the research register.] When I really understood nothing, I asked the AI to simplify so that I could still
make decisions, for example about which deeper paths to follow, and whether we needed more literature because the
agent was vague. I imagined the agent as an absolute professional mathematician who never bulges to my requests but
patiently works with me through the problems and explains them to me in a way that I, as a software engineer, can
ideally build a research framework around it, or tell it when I think we should hold, do more tests, do a review.
\item[Q7, deciding without checking the mathematics.] I discussed with Claude directly, had external AIs review the
papers and discussed with them; I asked them to take a hostile position and find errors, then compressed [their
findings] and gave them, with a context annotation, back to the original agent. Very similar to how real peer review
works, actually.
\item[Q8, effort and stopping.] I was pressing Fable at times more than it wanted, asserting that when it says ``this
is at least two weeks of work'' it is actually one to three hours for an AI, and demanding that it try. This worked,
and was particularly important, I think, in paper~IV.
\item[Q9, worst moment.] I did not really have a worst moment; [the closest was] running out of context, having burned
\$10, and needing to start again. I think it was about wanting too much rigour across, by then, four papers. One should
keep the context somewhat human, not absolutely superhuman. Restarts should, if possible, not happen in a dense
situation. They can happen because one runs out of money, or even because a sub-agent gets stuck in a loop and eats
away a lot. I think this is an open problem in general, but it was not a big problem during this process.
\item[Q10, own verification.] I looked at the code of some of the tests it implemented and was impressed by the
mathematical complexity. I let it run the tests often and ``reflect'' on them, and check for logical fallacies and
flawed results. I did not refuse anything, but I asked for excellence and simplicity, a kind of humbleness. This
worked a bit against me, even, as the model demanded more human input at times than was needed.
\item[Q11, authorship.] At first I even wanted it as co-author. The problem is that no journal will accept this. I
also think that, since it is not a persona, it is a bit weird. I do not fully know how to credit this, but I decided
to be absolutely open and transparent about everything, including the flow of work. This paper shall serve as a role
model for how to work scientifically in very complex domains while maintaining scientific rigour.
\item[Q12, this case study.] The audience is anybody working in science with AI, or wanting to, or even rejecting
it. I got a lot of hostility when presenting my work; nobody at first seriously checked it, assuming AI slop. I can
understand this. I have read the mathandai.org text, which is signed by almost all the Fields medallists, and I agree
with it. This project shall demonstrate a possible workable way, a toolkit, for embedding AI such that the quality of
its output rises and it becomes more easily falsifiable, borrowing the proven concept of test-driven development from
software engineering; enumerating and rating the toolsets are key concepts. I think this project is about the limits
of what AI can do; beyond it, it currently becomes infeasible. Already here, even experts will need a long time to
understand all of the output. Target venue: yes, why not the arXiv.
\end{description}

\section*{AI-assisted research: disclosure}
This case study was drafted by the same model that did the work it describes, from the repository record and an
interview with the author; the author verified every factual statement against the record and is responsible for
the text. The division of roles is the one stated in the disclosure sections of the four papers: the author posed
the questions, set the direction and the scope, and made every decision about what to pursue, claim and publish; the
model drafted arguments, wrote and ran the software, searched the literature and drafted the text, in dialogue with
the author.
